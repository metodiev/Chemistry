# Level 9b — Environmental Chemistry: Worked Solutions

> **Level:** 9b — Environmental Chemistry (advanced undergraduate / applied)
> **Accompanies:** [`../exercises/level-9b-environmental-exercises.md`](../exercises/level-9b-environmental-exercises.md)
> **Coverage:** [Ch. 1](../01-green-and-sustainable-chemistry.md), [Ch. 2](../02-atmospheric-chemistry.md), [Ch. 3](../03-water-chemistry-and-pollution.md), [Ch. 4](../04-waste-treatment.md)
> **Total: 250 marks.** 54 items, 28 of them multi-step.

## How these solutions are marked

Each solution carries five headings where they are relevant:

- **Strategy** — the relation or argument chosen before any substitution. This is
  where most of the marks sit in the longer items.
- **Working** — the arithmetic or the argument, with units carried through.
- **Answer** — the value to the justified number of significant figures, with
  units, or the key statement for a conceptual item.
- **Check** — an independent test that the answer is plausible: a balance, an
  order-of-magnitude estimate, or a limiting case.
- **Where marks are lost** — the specific error the examiner sees most often on
  that item.

**Standing assumptions.** Unless an item states otherwise, numerical answers
assume: ideal-gas behaviour; a well-mixed box where a box model is used; steady
state where a steady state is claimed; complete reaction or $100\ \%$ efficiency
where a stoichiometric calculation is requested; activities equal to
concentrations (dilute solution); and $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ for equilibrium
constants. Each solution names the assumption that dominates its uncertainty.

## Part A — Conceptual

### A1 — The twelve principles, grouped [Ch. 1] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Group by the *objective* the principle serves, then test the grouping
against a case where two principles genuinely conflict.

**Working.** (a) Reduce hazard: 3 (less hazardous synthesis), 4 (safer chemicals by
design), 5 (safer solvents and auxiliaries), 10 (design for degradation),
12 (inherently safer chemistry). Reduce material: 1 (prevention), 2 (atom economy),
7 (renewable feedstocks), 8 (fewer derivatives), 9 (catalysis). Reduce energy: 6
(energy efficiency), 11 (real-time analysis for pollution prevention).
(b) Principle 9 spans material and energy: a catalyst replaces a stoichiometric
reagent and usually lowers the operating temperature, so it cuts both mass and
energy.
(c) A renewable feedstock (7) may need energy-intensive purification (6): a bio-based
solvent produced by fermentation and purified by distillation can carry a larger
energy burden than the petrochemical solvent it replaces. The conflict is resolved
by a stated functional unit and boundary, with a sensitivity analysis on the energy
mix — not by ranking the principles.
(d) Prevention is a design instruction because it changes the synthesis, the
solvent, the separation and the product itself. It is addressed to the person
choosing the route, not to the person operating the waste contract.

**Answer.** (a) hazard 3, 4, 5, 10, 12; material 1, 2, 7, 8, 9; energy 6, 11.
(b) principle 9. (c) any conflict with a quantified resolution mechanism.
(d) prevention removes the waste from the process diagram rather than from the
effluent.

**Check.** All twelve principles appear exactly once. The grouping is interpretive,
not canonical; Chapter 1, Figure 1.1 makes the same point.

**Where marks are lost.** Reciting the principles in numerical order and calling
that a grouping; asserting a conflict without saying how it is resolved.

### A2 — Four green metrics [Ch. 1] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Define each metric by the question it answers and by what its
boundary contains.

**Working.** (a) Atom economy: what fraction of reactant mass is incorporated in the
product, from the balanced equation alone. E-factor: mass of waste per mass of
product, with the boundary stated. PMI: total mass input per mass of product,
numerically $E+1$. RME: product mass divided by the mass of reactants actually
charged, including excess and yield but not solvent unless the boundary says so.
(b) If the reaction is an addition, all reactant atoms enter the product, so
$\mathrm{AE} = 100\ \%$. The process can still use thirty volumes of a chlorinated
solvent and an aqueous work-up, so the waste mass is forty times the product mass
and $E = 40\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$.
(c) To a plant manager: PMI or E-factor, because they drive solvent, effluent and
disposal cost. To a regulator: the metric set with the boundary declared, and
hazard-weighted solvent data, because a single favourable number hides substitution
of one problem for another.

**Answer.** (a) as above. (b) solvent, work-up and purification dominate waste mass
while the reaction itself is perfectly atom-economical. (c) PMI/E-factor for the
plant; a declared boundary plus hazard data for the regulator.

**Check.** $\mathrm{PMI} = 40 + 1 = 41$ for the example in (b), consistent with the
definition $\mathrm{PMI} = E + 1$.

### A3 — Bio-based, biodegradable, compostable [Ch. 1] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Separate origin, fate and certification; then apply the design-for-
degradation argument to a chelating agent.

**Working.** (a) Bio-based describes the carbon source; biodegradable describes
microbial mineralisation; compostable requires a named standard and a defined
environment (temperature, humidity, time). Bio-based polyethylene is bio-based and
not biodegradable.
(b) EDTA is persistent and mobilises heavy metals from sediments; EDDS and GLDA
carry the same chelating function but are built with bonds that microorganisms can
cleave, so the ligand is mineralised rather than accumulated.
(c) Standard tests use a single inoculum, a high test concentration and a defined
temperature. A river may be cold, nutrient-poor, anoxic or deficient in the
specific organisms needed. A substance can pass a ready-biodegradability test by
primary degradation — losing the parent structure while the metabolite persists —
without achieving mineralisation.

**Answer.** The three properties are independent; degradation must be designed into
the molecule and verified in the receiving environment, not only in the test vessel.

**Where marks are lost.** Treating "bio-based" as a synonym for "biodegradable", and
treating "readily biodegradable" as a guarantee of environmental mineralisation.

### A4 — Atmospheric structure [Ch. 2] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Connect the temperature profile to the absorption of radiation at
different altitudes, then to stability and mixing.

**Working.** (a) Troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere. The tropopause
is the temperature minimum between the troposphere and the stratosphere, at about
8–18 km depending on latitude and season.
(b) The stratosphere is heated from within by ozone absorption of ultraviolet
radiation, so temperature rises with height and a displaced parcel is colder than
its surroundings and sinks back: statically stable, with little vertical mixing.
The troposphere is heated from below, cools with height, and is unstable enough to
sustain convection, clouds and weather.
(c) The tropopause is only a partial lid because convective overshooting, frontal
systems and the tropical tropopause temperature structure allow slow exchange. A
species emitted at the surface therefore has a tropospheric residence time of days
to weeks, but a stratospheric residence time of years.

**Answer.** The stability inversion at the tropopause is what separates a
fast-mixing lower atmosphere from a slowly mixed stratosphere.

### A5 — Chapman mechanism and its failure [Ch. 2] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Write the four reactions, then identify the missing loss term.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\ce{O2(g) ->[h\nu] 2O(g)}
\qquad
\ce{O(g) + O2(g) + M(g) -> O3(g) + M(g)}
$$

$$
\ce{O3(g) ->[h\nu] O2(g) + O(g)}
\qquad
\ce{O(g) + O3(g) -> 2O2(g)}
$$

The $\ce{O2}$ photolysis requires $\lambda < 242\ \text{nm}$; ozone photolysis
proceeds across the Hartley and Huggins bands and is the absorption that protects
the surface and heats the stratosphere.
(b) The mechanism contains only oxygen chemistry. It reproduces the shape and order
of magnitude of the ozone layer but overpredicts the column by about a factor of
two because it omits catalytic destruction by trace species — chlorine, bromine,
nitrogen and hydrogen radicals — which together carry roughly half the loss.
(c) $\ce{HCl}$ and $\ce{ClONO2}$ are chlorine reservoirs; $\ce{HNO3}$ is a nitrogen
reservoir. Converting a reservoir into an active radical releases the catalyst, so
the loss rate rises in proportion to the active concentration — and, for the dimer
cycle, in proportion to its square.

**Answer.** (a) the four reactions above; (b) missing catalytic loss;
(c) $\ce{HCl}$/$\ce{ClONO2}$ and $\ce{HNO3}$.

**Check.** Odd-oxygen bookkeeping: the first two reactions create two odd-oxygen
atoms from one $\ce{O2}$; the last two destroy odd oxygen. A mechanism with only
these four cannot close the budget.

### A6 — The ozone hole as a spring phenomenon [Ch. 2] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Link the vortex, the temperature, the phase of the chlorine and the
return of sunlight.

**Working.** (a) Winter produces the polar vortex and temperatures below about
195 K, so polar stratospheric clouds form. Heterogeneous chemistry on their
surfaces accumulates active chlorine in dark, reservoir-derived forms. When
sunlight returns in spring the chlorine is photolysed and rapid ozone loss begins;
by summer the vortex has broken down and ozone-rich air has mixed in.
(b)

$$
\ce{ClONO2(g) + HCl(s) -> Cl2(g) + HNO3(s)}
\qquad
\ce{ClONO2(g) + H2O(s) -> HOCl(g) + HNO3(s)}
$$

(c) Denitrification is the sedimentation of $\ce{HNO3}$-bearing cloud particles,
which removes nitrogen from the stratosphere. Without $\ce{NO2}$ the chlorine cannot
be re-sequestered as $\ce{ClONO2}$, so the catalytic cycle runs unchecked for weeks.

**Answer.** The hole requires cold, isolated, dark air to charge the chlorine
reservoir, and sunlight to discharge it.

### A7 — VOC-limited and NOx-limited regimes [Ch. 2] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Use the competition between $\ce{NO}$ titration of ozone and
peroxy-radical conversion of $\ce{NO}$ to $\ce{NO2}$.

**Working.** (a) In a VOC-limited regime, ozone production is limited by the supply
of peroxy radicals from VOC oxidation; in a NOx-limited regime it is limited by
$\ce{NO_x}$.
(b) Ozone is destroyed by $\ce{NO + O3 -> NO2 + O2}$ and produced by
$\ce{NO2 ->[h\nu] NO + O}$ followed by $\ce{O + O2 + M -> O3 + M}$. Cutting
$\ce{NO_x}$ in a VOC-limited city reduces the titration term faster than the
production term, so ozone rises.
(c) At weekends traffic falls; $\ce{NO_x}$ falls faster than VOC because diesel
$\ce{NO_x}$ dominates the weekday increment, moving the system towards NOx-limited
conditions and raising ozone. The diagnostic is a plot of ozone against
$\ce{NO_x}$ and VOC measurements, plus the weekday–weekend ratio; an isopleth model
is needed to attribute the regime.

**Answer.** (b) reduced titration; (c) the weekend effect is a regime shift, not a
measurement artefact.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing "less $\ce{NO_x}$, less ozone" as if the relation
were linear.

### A8 — Acid deposition [Ch. 2] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Compare the two oxidation pathways by their rates in the gas phase and
in the aqueous phase, then connect deposition to ecosystem buffering.

**Working.** (a) Gas phase: $\ce{SO2 + OH -> HSO3}$, followed by $\ce{HSO3 + O2 ->
SO3}$ and $\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}$. Aqueous phase: $\ce{SO2}$ dissolves and is
oxidised by $\ce{H2O2}$ (fast, pH-dependent), by $\ce{O3}$, or catalytically by
Fe/Mn. Inside cloud droplets the aqueous $\ce{H2O2}$ pathway usually dominates.
(b) The critical load is the deposition rate below which no harmful effect occurs
for a defined ecosystem. It depends on the buffering capacity of the soil and
bedrock: granite and soft-water lakes have little alkalinity and low critical loads;
limestone soils are buffered by carbonate dissolution and tolerate much more.
(c) Sulfate stored in soil can be released for decades after deposition falls, so
the soil solution remains acidic, aluminium stays mobile, and lake chemistry
recovers more slowly than emissions decline.

**Answer.** (a) aqueous $\ce{H2O2}$ oxidation dominates in cloud; (b) ecosystem-
specific threshold; (c) stored sulfate and slow soil recovery.

### A9 — pH, alkalinity, hardness [Ch. 3] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** Separate intensity (pH), capacity (alkalinity) and composition
(hardness).

**Working.** (a) pH is $-\log a_{\ce{H+}}$, dimensionless. Alkalinity is the
acid-neutralising capacity, in $\text{meq dm}^{-3}$ or $\text{mg dm}^{-3}$ as
$\ce{CaCO3}$. Hardness is the sum of calcium and magnesium, also reported as
$\text{mg dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{CaCO3}$ but a different quantity.
(b) In an open system the water exchanges $\ce{CO2}$ with a fixed atmospheric
partial pressure (a lake, a river). In a closed system the total inorganic carbon
is fixed and no gas exchange occurs (a sealed vessel, a confined aquifer).
(c) At pH 7.0 a water may contain almost no bicarbonate: the pH is set by a small
amount of strong acid or base, and the alkalinity — the capacity to absorb more
acid — is nearly zero.

**Answer.** (b) open: fixed $p_{\ce{CO2}}$; closed: fixed $C_T$; (c) no buffer
capacity despite neutral pH.

**Where marks are lost.** Reporting hardness and alkalinity interchangeably because
both are expressed as $\ce{CaCO3}$.

### A10 — The waste hierarchy [Ch. 4] (2 marks)

**Strategy.** State the hierarchy, then give cases where the ranking is overturned
by a life-cycle comparison.

**Working.** (a) Prevention, reuse, recycling, recovery, disposal.
(b) Reuse of a heavy glass container that must be transported several hundred
kilometres can carry a larger burden than local recycling; recycling of a
contaminated mixed plastic into a low-grade product can displace less virgin
material than energy recovery displaces fossil electricity. Both are decided by a
functional unit and a boundary, not by the hierarchy.
(c) Extended producer responsibility transfers end-of-life cost and responsibility
to the producer, so a design that is hard to dismantle or contains an inseparable
laminate becomes a liability rather than an externality; the producer then
simplifies the material mix and designs for disassembly.

**Answer.** The hierarchy ranks options by the *potential* to avoid burden, not by
the realised burden; (c) internalises the end-of-life cost.

## Part B — Basic problems

### B11 — Atom economy of toluene nitration [Ch. 1] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** $\mathrm{AE} = M_\text{product}/\sum\nu_iM_\text{reactants}$, with
stoichiometric coefficients of one throughout.

**Working.**
$M_\text{reactants} = 92.14 + 63.01 = 155.15\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.
$M_\text{product} = 137.14\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

$$
\mathrm{AE} = \frac{137.14}{155.15}\times100 = 88.39\ \%.
$$

**Answer.** $\mathrm{AE} = 88.4\ \%$ (3 s.f.). It is a property of the balanced
equation, not of the process: yield, solvent and work-up do not enter.

**Check.** The only discarded mass is water, $18.02\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, so
$\mathrm{AE} = 1 - 18.02/155.15 = 88.4\ \%$. The two routes agree.

**Assumption.** Unit stoichiometric coefficients and complete conversion. The
dominant uncertainty is conceptual, not analytical: atom economy ignores the excess
nitric acid actually charged, so the process figure will be lower.

**Where marks are lost.** Including the water as a reactant, or using the charged
mass of nitric acid instead of the stoichiometric mass.

### B12 — E-factor and PMI [Ch. 1] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** $E = m_\text{waste}/m_\text{product}$ and $\mathrm{PMI} = E + 1$,
with both masses inside the same boundary.

**Working.**

$$
E = \frac{3000}{250} = 12.0\ \text{kg kg}^{-1},
\qquad
\mathrm{PMI} = 12.0 + 1 = 13.0\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}.
$$

**Answer.** $E = 12.0\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$; $\mathrm{PMI} = 13.0\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$,
for a plant-gate boundary that includes solvent and aqueous streams but excludes
upstream feedstock production and downstream use.

**Assumption.** All material input leaves as product or waste; nothing accumulates
in the plant.

**Check.** $\mathrm{PMI} = m_\text{input}/m_\text{product} = (3000+250)/250 = 13.0$,
consistent.

**Where marks are lost.** Reporting PMI as $E$ (out by one), or failing to state the
boundary — an E-factor without a boundary is not a result.

### B13 — Atmospheric residence time [Ch. 2] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** $\tau = M/F$ with both quantities in the same mass unit.

**Working.**

$$
\tau = \frac{3500\ \text{Tg}}{500\ \text{Tg yr}^{-1}} = 7.0\ \text{yr}.
$$

(b) $\ce{CO2}$ is not removed by a single first-order process. It exchanges rapidly
with the surface ocean and biosphere, and a fraction of an emitted pulse remains
for millennia because the slow reservoirs (deep ocean, carbonate sediments) control
the tail. A single burden-over-flux ratio therefore describes an instantaneous
turnover, not the lifetime of an emission; climate policy uses cumulative emissions
and impulse-response functions instead.

**Answer.** $\tau = 7.0\ \text{yr}$. The methane value is a genuine lifetime; the
carbon dioxide equivalent is not.

**Assumption.** Steady state: burden is constant, so source equals sink.

**Check.** Seven years is consistent with the hydroxyl-radical sink for methane and
is short enough that emission cuts show within a decade.

### B14 — ppb to mass concentration [Ch. 2] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** $c = \chi P/(RT)$ in $\text{mol m}^{-3}$, then multiply by the molar
mass.

**Working.** At 298 K and $101\,325\ \text{Pa}$:

$$
c = 40\times10^{-9}\times\frac{101\,325}{8.314\times298}
= 1.635\times10^{-6}\ \text{mol m}^{-3},
$$

$$
\rho = 1.635\times10^{-6}\times48.00 = 7.85\times10^{-5}\ \text{g m}^{-3}
= 78.5\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}.
$$

At 273.15 K the same mole fraction gives

$$
\rho = 40\times10^{-9}\times\frac{101\,325}{8.314\times273.15}\times48.00
= 85.7\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}.
$$

(c) For $\ce{NO2}$ at 298 K the factor is
$101\,325/(8.314\times298)\times46.01\times10^{3} = 1.88\
\mu\text{g m}^{-3}$ per ppb. Because the factor is proportional to $P/T$, the same
ppb value corresponds to different mass concentrations at different temperatures —
about $9\ \%$ higher at 0 °C than at 25 °C.

**Answer.** (a) $78.5\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}$; (b) $85.7\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}$;
(c) $1.88\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}$ per ppb of $\ce{NO2}$ at 298 K.

**Assumption.** Ideal gas; air behaves as a single ideal gas at the stated $P$ and
$T$.

**Check.** The ratio $85.7/78.5 = 1.092$ equals $298/273.15 = 1.091$, as required.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $22.4\ \text{dm}^{3}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ at 25 °C, or
converting with a factor quoted for a different temperature.

### B15 — Hardness as CaCO₃ [Ch. 3] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** Sum the divalent concentrations and multiply by the equivalent molar
mass of $\ce{CaCO3}$, $100.09\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

**Working.**

$$
[\ce{Ca^{2+}}] + [\ce{Mg^{2+}}] = 1.50 + 0.50 = 2.00\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3},
$$

$$
\mathrm{Hardness} = 2.00\times100.09 = 200.2\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}\ \text{as } \ce{CaCO3}.
$$

In German degrees:

$$
200.2/17.85 = 11.2\ ^{\circ}\text{dH}.
$$

**Answer.** $2.00\times10^{2}\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ as $\ce{CaCO3}$, or
$11.2\ ^{\circ}\text{dH}$; "very hard" (above $180\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$).

**Assumption.** Strontium and barium are negligible; the water is dilute, so
activity corrections are unnecessary.

**Check.** Each millimole of divalent ion corresponds to $100.09\ \text{mg}$ of
$\ce{CaCO3}$; one mole of $\ce{CaCO3}$ has two equivalents, so the arithmetic is a
direct equivalence and not a mass of calcium.

**Where marks are lost.** Multiplying by $40.08$ (the mass of calcium) instead of
$100.09$ (the mass of the $\ce{CaCO3}$ equivalent).

### B16 — BOD₅ without a seed correction [Ch. 3] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** $\mathrm{BOD}_5 = (D_1-D_2)/P$ with $P$ as a volume fraction.

**Working.**

$$
\mathrm{BOD}_5 = \frac{8.4-4.9}{0.025} = \frac{3.5}{0.025}
= 140\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

**Answer.** $1.4\times10^{2}\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ (2 s.f.).

**Check.** The depletion is $3.5\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$, inside the usual $2$–$6\
\text{mg dm}^{-3}$ window, and the residual $4.9\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ is well above
$1\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$; the dilution is therefore valid. A value near
$140\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ is typical of a settled but untreated domestic wastewater.

**Assumption.** Unseeded sample, dark incubation at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$, no
nitrification inhibitor omitted. The dominant uncertainty is the dilution factor,
which enters the answer directly and is often known to only two significant
figures.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $P = 2.5$ instead of $0.025$, which gives
$1.4\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ — a physically impossible answer for a strong sewage.

### B17 — Alkalinity from bicarbonate [Ch. 3] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** Each bicarbonate carries one acid-neutralising equivalent; convert to
$\ce{CaCO3}$ with the equivalent mass $50.04\ \text{g eq}^{-1}$.

**Working.**

$$
\mathrm{Alk} = 2.40\ \text{meq dm}^{-3},
$$

$$
\mathrm{Alk}\ \text{as } \ce{CaCO3} = 2.40\times50.04
= 120.1\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

**Answer.** $2.40\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$ or $120\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ as $\ce{CaCO3}$
(3 s.f.).

**Check.** The equivalent mass of $\ce{CaCO3}$ is half its molar mass,
$100.09/2 = 50.04$, because each carbonate neutralises two protons. Multiplying
$2.40$ by $100.09$ would overstate the alkalinity by a factor of two.

**Assumption.** Carbonate and hydroxide are negligible at pH 7.8 and no other weak
acids are present. The dominant uncertainty is the unmeasured non-carbonate
alkalinity — organic acids and silicate — which a titration would include and this
calculation cannot.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the full molar mass of $\ce{CaCO3}$ for a
monovalent species such as bicarbonate.

### B18 — Toxic equivalent of a dioxin mixture [Ch. 4] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** $\mathrm{TEQ} = \sum c_i\mathrm{TEF}_i$; the sum is over congeners and
the units are those of the concentration.

**Working.**

$$
\mathrm{TEQ} = 1.5(1.0) + 4.0(1.0) + 6.0(0.3) + 12(0.1) + 9.0(0.1) + 200(0.0003),
$$

$$
\mathrm{TEQ} = 1.5 + 4.0 + 1.8 + 1.2 + 0.9 + 0.06 = 9.46\ \text{ng TEQ g}^{-1}.
$$

**Answer.** $9.5\ \text{ng TEQ g}^{-1}$ (2 s.f.).

**Check.** OCDD is the most abundant congener by mass ($200\ \text{ng g}^{-1}$ of a
total $232.5\ \text{ng g}^{-1}$, or $86\ \%$) but contributes only $0.06$, or
$0.6\ \%$ of the TEQ.
The TEQ is dominated by the two TEF-1.0 congeners. A total-mass result would be
meaningless.

**Assumption.** The 2005 WHO TEF scheme; TEFs differ between schemes, so the scheme
must be stated.

**Where marks are lost.** Reporting the arithmetic mean TEF, or omitting the OCDD
term because it is "negligible" — it is small but not zero, and omitting it loses
the precision mark.

### B19 — Radiative forcing of CO₂ [Ch. 2] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** Insert the ratio into the logarithmic forcing expression; the ratio is
dimensionless, so ppm or Pa give the same answer.

**Working.**

$$
\Delta F = 5.35\ln\!\left(\frac{420}{280}\right)
= 5.35\ln(1.5) = 5.35\times0.4055 = 2.17\ \text{W m}^{-2}.
$$

(b) The strong absorption bands of $\ce{CO2}$ at $667\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ and
$2349\ \text{cm}^{-1}$ are already close to saturation at the band centre; added
$\ce{CO2}$ acts mainly on the wings of the bands, where the atmosphere is still
partly transparent. Each additional molecule therefore absorbs a smaller increment
of outgoing radiation, and the forcing grows as the logarithm of concentration.
(c) $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ are homonuclear and symmetric: their vibrational modes
do not change the dipole moment, so they do not absorb infrared radiation. $\ce{CO2}$
(bending mode) and $\ce{CH4}$ (four modes, two infrared-active) change the dipole
moment when they vibrate.

**Answer.** $\Delta F = 2.17\ \text{W m}^{-2}$; a forcing, not a temperature.

**Check.** The value matches the accepted forcing for the 280 → 420 ppm increase.
Converting to temperature requires a climate sensitivity of roughly $0.5$–$0.8\
\text{K per W m}^{-2}$ and is not part of the question.

**Assumption.** The logarithmic approximation holds over this concentration range
and no other forcing agent changes. The dominant uncertainty is not the forcing but
the climate sensitivity used to convert it to a temperature change.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $\log_{10}$ instead of $\ln$, which gives
$0.94\ \text{W m}^{-2}$ and is wrong by a factor of $2.3$.

### B20 — Lime requirement for leachate [Ch. 4] (3 marks)

**Strategy.** Convert acid equivalents to moles, divide by two because each
$\ce{Ca(OH)2}$ supplies two hydroxide ions, then apply the molar mass.

**Working.**
$n_{\ce{H+}} = 50\ \text{m}^{3}\times10^{3}\ \text{dm}^{3}\ \text{m}^{-3}\times
0.020\ \text{mol dm}^{-3} = 1000\ \text{mol}$.

$$
n_{\ce{Ca(OH)2}} = 1000/2 = 500\ \text{mol},
\qquad
m = 500\times74.09 = 37\,045\ \text{g} = 37.0\ \text{kg}.
$$

With $10\ \%$ excess: $37.0\times1.10 = 40.7\ \text{kg}$.

(c) Real leachate contains volatile fatty acids and dissolved metals that consume
lime without appearing in a strong-acid titration, and the plant controls to a
target pH rather than to a strong-acid equivalence. The dose is therefore set by
titration of the actual batch, and is typically higher.

**Answer.** (a) $37.0\ \text{kg}$; (b) $40.7\ \text{kg}$; (c) weak acids, metal
hydrolysis and the pH endpoint.

**Assumption.** The stated acidity is a strong-acid equivalence and the lime is
$100\ \%$ reactive.

**Check.** $37\ \text{kg}$ in $50\ \text{m}^{3}$ is $0.74\ \text{g dm}^{-3}$,
plausible for a mildly acidic leachate.

## Part C — Intermediate problems

### C21 — A four-metric comparison of two epoxidation routes [Ch. 1] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Compute AE from the equations (intrinsic), then E, PMI and RME from
the process masses (boundary-dependent), and compare like with like.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\mathrm{AE_A} = \frac{58.08}{42.08+70.90+74.09}\times100 = \frac{58.08}{187.07}\times100
= 31.0\ \%;
$$

$$
\mathrm{AE_B} = \frac{58.08}{42.08+34.01}\times100 = \frac{58.08}{76.09}\times100
= 76.3\ \%.
$$

(b) Total input $= $ reactants $+$ solvent $+$ water; waste $=$ input $-$ product.

Route A: input $= 3400+8000+2000 = 13\,400\ \text{kg}$; waste
$= 12\,400\ \text{kg}$.

$$
E_\text{A} = \frac{12\,400}{1000} = 12.4\ \text{kg kg}^{-1},
\quad
\mathrm{PMI_A} = 13.4\ \text{kg kg}^{-1},
\quad
\mathrm{RME_A} = \frac{1000}{3400}\times100 = 29.4\ \%.
$$

Route B: input $= 1420+1500+500 = 3420\ \text{kg}$; waste
$= 2420\ \text{kg}$.

$$
E_\text{B} = 2.42\ \text{kg kg}^{-1},
\quad
\mathrm{PMI_B} = 3.42\ \text{kg kg}^{-1},
\quad
\mathrm{RME_B} = \frac{1000}{1420}\times100 = 70.4\ \%.
$$

(c) PMI and E-factor carry the decision, because they measure the material the plant
must handle, heat, separate and dispose of. The atom economy alone would understate
the advantage: $31\ \%$ against $76\ \%$ is a factor of $2.5$, while the process
intensity differs by a factor of $3.9$. Reporting only atom economy would also hide
that Route B still generates $2.4\ \text{kg}$ of waste per kilogram of product.

**Answer.** AE $31.0\ \%$ vs $76.3\ \%$; $E$ $12.4$ vs $2.42\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$;
PMI $13.4$ vs $3.42\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$; RME $29.4\ \%$ vs $70.4\ \%$.

**Assumption.** Plant-gate boundary; solvent not recovered; all inputs leave as
product or waste.

**Check.** For each route, waste $+$ product $=$ input, and
$\mathrm{PMI} = E+1$. Both hold to the rounding shown.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the charged reactant mass (3400 kg) inside the atom
economy, or omitting water from the PMI while including it in the E-factor.

### C22 — Carbon efficiency with and without recycle [Ch. 1] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Follow the carbon: feedstock in, product out, $\ce{CO2}$ out, and
unreacted feedstock either lost or recycled.

**Working.** Per mole of feedstock, 9 mol of carbon enter the product and 1 mol
leaves as $\ce{CO2}$ at complete conversion. At $85\ \%$ yield:

- product carbon $= 0.85\times9 = 7.65\ \text{mol}$;
- $\ce{CO2}$ carbon $= 0.85\times1 = 0.85\ \text{mol}$;
- unreacted feedstock carbon $= 0.15\times10 = 1.50\ \text{mol}$.

Check the balance: $7.65+0.85+1.50 = 10.0$.

(a) Without recycle:

$$
C_\text{eff} = \frac{7.65}{10}\times100 = 76.5\ \%.
$$

(b) With recycle to extinction, the unreacted feedstock re-enters and no feedstock
carbon is lost:

$$
C_\text{eff} = \frac{7.65}{7.65+0.85}\times100 = 90.0\ \%.
$$

(c) Carbon efficiency counts carbon atoms, whereas yield counts moles of product.
They coincide only when the product contains all the carbon of the feedstock, as
in an isomerisation; here one carbon per molecule is deliberately discarded as
$\ce{CO2}$, so even a quantitative reaction cannot exceed $90\ \%$.

**Answer.** (a) $76.5\ \%$; (b) $90.0\ \%$; (c) the $\ce{CO2}$ by-product.

**Assumption.** All feedstock carbon is accounted for by product, $\ce{CO2}$ and
unreacted feedstock; no carbon is incorporated into biomass or lost as other
by-products.

**Check.** The recycled value $90.0\ \%$ equals the stoichiometric ceiling
$9/10 = 90\ \%$. A calculation exceeding it would signal a bookkeeping error.

### C23 — The Leighton relationship [Ch. 2] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Use
$[\ce{O3}][\ce{NO}]/[\ce{NO2}] = J_{\ce{NO2}}/k$, rearrange for $[\ce{O3}]$, then
convert molecules $\text{cm}^{-3}$ to a mole fraction.

**Working.** (a)

$$
[\ce{O3}] = \frac{J}{k}\cdot\frac{[\ce{NO2}]}{[\ce{NO}]}
= \frac{6.0\times10^{-3}}{1.8\times10^{-14}}\times3.0
= 1.0\times10^{12}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}.
$$

Number density of air at 298 K and $101\,325\ \text{Pa}$:

$$
\frac{N}{V} = \frac{P}{RT}N_A
= \frac{101\,325}{8.314\times298}\times6.022\times10^{23}
= 2.46\times10^{19}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}.
$$

$$
\chi_{\ce{O3}} = \frac{1.0\times10^{12}}{2.46\times10^{19}}
= 4.06\times10^{-8} = 40.6\ \text{ppb}.
$$

(b) $\tau_{\ce{NO2}} = 1/J = 1/(6.0\times10^{-3}) = 167\ \text{s}$.

(c) Doubling the ratio doubles $[\ce{O3}]$: $2.0\times10^{12}\
\text{molecules cm}^{-3} = 81.2\ \text{ppb}$.

(d) VOCs supply peroxy radicals that convert $\ce{NO}$ to $\ce{NO2}$ without
destroying ozone ($\ce{RO2 + NO -> RO + NO2}$, $\ce{HO2 + NO -> OH + NO2}$). The
photostationary state is displaced: $\ce{NO2}$ is replenished by a route that does
not consume $\ce{O3}$, so ozone accumulates above the Leighton value.

**Answer.** (a) $1.0\times10^{12}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}$, $40.6\ \text{ppb}$;
(b) $167\ \text{s}$; (c) $81.2\ \text{ppb}$; (d) peroxy-radical conversion of
$\ce{NO}$.

**Assumption.** Photostationary state with $\ce{NO_x}$ only, no VOC input and no
heterogeneous loss; ideal gas at 298 K.

**Check.** A two-minute $\ce{NO2}$ photolysis lifetime means the cycle turns over
roughly $500$ times per daylight hour, which is why ozone responds within minutes
to a change in sunlight.

**Where marks are lost.** Mixing $\text{cm}^{-3}$ with $\text{m}^{-3}$ in the
number-density conversion, which gives an answer wrong by $10^{6}$.

### C24 — The chlorine dimer cycle [Ch. 2] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Add the three steps so the catalyst cancels, then note that two ozone
molecules are lost per dimer formed.

**Working.** (a) Adding

$$
\ce{2ClO + M -> Cl2O2 + M},
\qquad
\ce{Cl2O2 ->[h\nu] 2Cl + O2},
\qquad
\ce{2Cl + 2O3 -> 2ClO + 2O2}
$$

gives $\ce{2O3 -> 3O2}$. Chlorine appears on both sides and is not consumed.

(b) The dimer formation rate is pseudo-second order in $\ce{ClO}$:

$$
r_\text{dimer} = k[\ce{ClO}]^{2} = 2.0\times10^{-14}\times(1.0\times10^{10})^{2}
= 2.0\times10^{6}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

Each dimer destroyed removes two ozone molecules, so at steady state

$$
-\frac{\mathrm{d}[\ce{O3}]}{\mathrm{d}t}\bigg|_\text{dimer}
= 2r_\text{dimer} = 4.0\times10^{6}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

The single-chlorine cycle $\ce{Cl + O3 -> ClO + O2}$ gives

$$
r_{\ce{Cl}} = 1.2\times10^{-11}\times2.0\times10^{4}\times4.0\times10^{12}
= 9.6\times10^{5}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1},
$$

and the $\ce{ClO + O}$ cycle gives

$$
r_{\ce{ClO+O}} = 3.8\times10^{-11}\times1.0\times10^{10}\times1.0\times10^{6}
= 3.8\times10^{5}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

(c) Total $= (4.0+0.96+0.38)\times10^{6} = 5.34\times10^{6}\
\text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The dimer share is
$4.0/5.34 = 74.9\ \%$.

$$
\text{fractional loss per day} = \frac{4.0\times10^{6}\times86\,400}{4.0\times10^{12}}
= 8.6\ \%\ \text{d}^{-1}.
$$

Per chlorine atom, $4.0\times10^{6}/1.0\times10^{10} = 4.0\times10^{-4}\
\text{s}^{-1}$, or $34.6$ ozone molecules per day.

(d) Halving $[\ce{ClO}]$ quarters the rate:
$2.0\times10^{-14}\times(5.0\times10^{9})^{2} = 5.0\times10^{5}$, so ozone loss
falls to $1.0\times10^{6}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$, or $2.2\ \%\
\text{d}^{-1}$. A $30\ \%$ fall in chlorine produces a $51\ \%$ fall in the dimer
loss: the response is quadratic, which is why the hole grew so fast and why it is
closing slowly.

**Answer.** (a) $\ce{2O3 -> 3O2}$; (b) $4.0\times10^{6}$, $9.6\times10^{5}$,
$3.8\times10^{5}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$; (c) $74.9\ \%$,
$8.6\ \%$ per day; (d) $1.0\times10^{6}$, quadratic response.

**Assumption.** Steady state for $\ce{Cl2O2}$ (formation equals photolysis); unit
quantum yield for $\ce{Cl2O2}$ photolysis; no heterogeneous loss of $\ce{ClONO2}$.

**Check.** The three rates are within one order of magnitude of each other, which is
what "comparable contributions" means in the lower stratosphere; and the fractional
loss of a few per cent per day is consistent with a springtime column depletion
over several weeks.

**Where marks are lost.** Forgetting the factor of two — one dimer destroys two
ozone molecules — and reporting $2.0\times10^{6}$ as the ozone loss.

### C25 — The pH of polluted rain [Ch. 2] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Convert each ion to equivalents, sum the acid anions, subtract the
neutralising ammonium, then take $-\log_{10}$ of the net concentration.

**Working.** (a) Sulfate, expressed as $\ce{SO4^{2-}}$, carries two equivalents per
mole:

$$
\frac{6.0}{96.06}\times2 = 0.1249\ \text{meq dm}^{-3};
$$

nitrate carries one:

$$
\frac{3.0}{62.00}\times1 = 0.0484\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}.
$$

Total acid $= 0.1733\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$.

(b) Ammonium neutralises one equivalent per mole:

$$
\frac{0.9}{18.04} = 0.0499\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}.
$$

Without ammonium:

$$
[\ce{H+}] = 1.733\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3},
\qquad \mathrm{pH} = 3.76.
$$

With ammonium: net $= 0.1733-0.0499 = 0.1234\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$,

$$
[\ce{H+}] = 1.234\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3},
\qquad \mathrm{pH} = 3.91.
$$

(c) Relative to pH 5.6:

$$
\frac{[\ce{H+}]_{3.91}}{[\ce{H+}]_{5.6}} = 10^{5.6-3.91} = 49.
$$

The sample is about fifty times more acidic than clean rain.

(d) Sulfate, because sulfuric acid is less volatile than nitric acid, so it is
retained in aerosol and in surface deposits; nitrate partitions back to
$\ce{HNO3(g)}$ and is more readily re-emitted.

**Answer.** (a) $0.173\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$; (b) pH 3.76 (unneutralised), pH 3.91
(observed); (c) about $49\times$; (d) sulfate.

**Assumption.** Strong-acid behaviour: both acids fully dissociated, and ammonium
acts as a pure base; no organic acids, which would lower the pH further.

**Check.** The concentrations are internally consistent: $0.123\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}
= 1.23\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$, and pH $3.91$ corresponds to
$1.2\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

**Where marks are lost.** Treating sulfate as monovalent, which halves the acid
equivalent and shifts the pH by $0.3$ units.

### C26 — PM₂.₅ mass closure and aerosol acidity [Ch. 2] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Sum the measured components for the mass, then compare the ammonium
charge with the charge required by sulfate and nitrate.

**Working.** (a)

$$
m_{\text{PM}_{2.5}} = 5.2+3.1+2.4+4.0+1.8+1.9 = 18.4\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}.
$$

Secondary inorganic aerosol $= 5.2+3.1+2.4 = 10.7\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}$, a fraction

$$
\frac{10.7}{18.4} = 58.2\ \%.
$$

(b) Charge required, in $\mu\text{eq m}^{-3}$:

$$
\text{sulfate} = \frac{5.2}{96.06}\times2\times10^{3} = 108.3,
\qquad
\text{nitrate} = \frac{3.1}{62.00}\times10^{3} = 50.0,
$$

$$
\text{required} = 158.3\ \mu\text{eq m}^{-3};
\qquad
\text{ammonium available} = \frac{2.4}{18.04}\times10^{3} = 133.0\ \mu\text{eq m}^{-3}.
$$

The ammonium supplies $133.0/158.3 = 84\ \%$ of the neutralising charge, so the

(c) $18.4/5 = 3.7$, so the site exceeds the annual guideline by a factor of $3.7$.

require an assumption about the cation balance that the measurement does not

**Answer.** (a) $18.4\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}$, $58.2\ \%$ secondary inorganic;
(b) $84\ \%$ neutralised — ammonium-poor, acidic; (c) $3.7\times$ the guideline;
(d) the counter-ion is unknown.

**Assumption.** The filter mass is the sum of the analysed components, with no
undetected water or oxygen associated with the measured ions; measurement
uncertainty is not propagated.

**Check.** If the ammonium had exceeded the required charge, the excess would have
to be balanced by chloride or another anion — a useful consistency test on any
aerosol composition table.

### C27 — Open-system carbonate equilibrium [Ch. 3] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** At fixed alkalinity, carbonate alkalinity is
$\mathrm{Alk} = [\ce{HCO3^-}] + 2[\ce{CO3^{2-}}]$, and the two species are linked by
$K_{a2}$; solve for $[\ce{HCO3^-}]$ first.

**Working.** (a) With $[\ce{H+}] = 10^{-8.10} = 7.94\times10^{-9}$:

$$
\frac{[\ce{CO3^{2-}}]}{[\ce{HCO3^-}]} = \frac{K_{a2}}{[\ce{H+}]}
= \frac{4.7\times10^{-11}}{7.94\times10^{-9}} = 5.92\times10^{-3}.
$$

$$
[\ce{HCO3^-}] = \frac{1.80\times10^{-3}}{1+2(5.92\times10^{-3})}
= 1.779\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3},
$$

$$
[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] = 1.779\times10^{-3}\times5.92\times10^{-3}
= 1.05\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

(b)

$$
[\ce{H2CO3^*}] = \frac{[\ce{H+}][\ce{HCO3^-}]}{K_{a1}}
= \frac{7.94\times10^{-9}\times1.779\times10^{-3}}{4.3\times10^{-7}}
= 3.29\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

$$
C_T = 3.29\times10^{-5}+1.779\times10^{-3}+1.05\times10^{-5}
= 1.82\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.
$$

(c)

$$
p_{\ce{CO2}} = \frac{[\ce{H2CO3^*}]}{K_H} = \frac{3.29\times10^{-5}}{3.4\times10^{-2}}
= 9.67\times10^{-4}\ \text{atm} = 967\ \mu\text{atm}.
$$

This is $967/420 = 2.30$ times the present atmospheric value.

(d) The water is supersaturated in $\ce{CO2}$ relative to the atmosphere, so it will
degas. Removing $\ce{CO2}$ shifts the carbonate equilibria towards carbonate and
consumes hydrogen ion, so the pH rises slowly downstream — the same direction as
photosynthetic carbon uptake in a productive lake.

**Answer.** (a) $[\ce{HCO3^-}] = 1.78\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}$,
$[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] = 10.5\ \mu\text{mol dm}^{-3}$; (b) $C_T = 1.82\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}$;
(c) $967\ \mu\text{atm}$, $2.3\times$ atmospheric; (d) degassing, pH rises.

**Assumption.** Closed to gas exchange at the moment of sampling (the alkalinity is
conserved), activities equal concentrations, $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$, no organic
alkalinity and no other weak acids.

**Check.** $\mathrm{Alk} = 1.779\times10^{-3} + 2(1.05\times10^{-5}) =
1.80\times10^{-3}\ \text{eq dm}^{-3}$, which reproduces the input exactly.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the total inorganic carbon instead of the alkalinity
as the constraint, which is the closed-system calculation and gives a different pH.

### C28 — Closed-system carbonic acid [Ch. 3] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** In a closed system $C_T$ is fixed; the charge balance supplies the
second equation.

**Working.** (a) Electroneutrality requires

$$
[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{OH^-}] + [\ce{HCO3^-}] + 2[\ce{CO3^{2-}}].
$$

At the acidic pH that dissolved $\ce{CO2}$ produces, $[\ce{OH^-}]$ is negligible and
$[\ce{CO3^{2-}}]$ is suppressed by $K_{a2}/[\ce{H+}] \ll 1$, so
$[\ce{H+}] \approx [\ce{HCO3^-}]$.

(b) With $[\ce{HCO3^-}] = C_T K_{a1}/([\ce{H+}]+K_{a1})$ and
$[\ce{H+}] = [\ce{HCO3^-}]$:

$$
[\ce{H+}]^{2} + K_{a1}[\ce{H+}] - C_TK_{a1} = 0.
$$

$$
[\ce{H+}] = \frac{-4.3\times10^{-7} + \sqrt{(4.3\times10^{-7})^{2}
+ 4(2.0\times10^{-3})(4.3\times10^{-7})}}{2}
= 2.91\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3},
$$

$$
\mathrm{pH} = 4.54.
$$

(c) At this pH, $[\ce{H+}] \gg K_{a1}$, so
$\alpha_1 = [\ce{HCO3^-}]/C_T = 2.91\times10^{-5}/2.0\times10^{-3} = 1.46\ \%$ and
$\alpha_0 = 98.5\ \%$. Carbonate is negligible
($\alpha_2 \approx 2\times10^{-8}$).

(d) A closed water at the same $C_T$ cannot lose $\ce{CO2}$, so the acid produced by
dissociation accumulates and suppresses further dissociation. An open water at the
same $p_{\ce{CO2}}$ has an effectively infinite reservoir: the $\ce{H+}$ produced is
buffered by the atmosphere, the dissociation is driven further, and the pH settles
near 5.6 rather than 4.5.

**Answer.** (b) pH 4.54; (c) $\ce{HCO3^-}$ $1.5\ \%$, $\ce{CO2(aq)+H2CO3}$
$98.5\ \%$; (d) no gas exchange, so $\ce{H+}$ accumulates.

**Assumption.** Pure $\ce{CO2}$ in pure water, no strong acid or base, no metal
cations, $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

**Check.** $[\ce{H+}]\approx[\ce{HCO3^-}]=2.9\times10^{-5}$ and
$\mathrm{pH}+\mathrm{pOH}=14.00$. Both hold.

### C29 — BOD₅, COD and the biodegradability ratio [Ch. 3] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Apply the seeded BOD equation with the dilution as a fraction, then
use the dichromate–iron stoichiometry for COD.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\mathrm{BOD}_5 = \frac{(8.8-3.1)-0.85(8.5-7.6)}{0.030}
= \frac{5.7-0.765}{0.030} = \frac{4.935}{0.030}
= 164.5\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

(b) Iron(II) consumed by the dichromate:

$$
\Delta V = 24.60-15.40 = 9.20\ \text{cm}^{3},
\qquad
n_{\ce{Fe}} = 9.20\times10^{-3}\times0.1000 = 9.20\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}.
$$

Six iron(II) reduce one dichromate, so
$n_{\ce{Cr2O7^{2-}}} = 9.20\times10^{-4}/6 = 1.533\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol}$.

Each dichromate accepts six electrons, and each $\ce{O2}$ accepts four, so the
oxygen equivalent is $6/4 = 1.5$:

$$
n_{\ce{O2}} = 1.5\times1.533\times10^{-4} = 2.30\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol in } 0.0200\ \text{dm}^{3};
$$

$$
\mathrm{COD} = \frac{2.30\times10^{-4}\times32.00}{0.0200}\times10^{3}
= 368\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

(c)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{BOD}_5}{\mathrm{COD}} = \frac{164.5}{368} = 0.447.
$$

A ratio near $0.45$ indicates a substantial biodegradable fraction with a
significant non-biodegradable residue: the effluent will respond to biological
treatment but will not be fully mineralised by it.

(d) COD oxidises everything that dichromate can oxidise under reflux, including
non-biodegradable organics, reduced inorganic species and some aromatic material.
BOD measures only what the microbial consortium consumes over five days. COD is
therefore always at least as large as the ultimate BOD, and the ratio is a measure
of biodegradability rather than of strength.

**Answer.** (a) $1.6\times10^{2}\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$; (b) $368\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$
as $\ce{O2}$; (c) $0.45$, moderately biodegradable; (d) different oxidant scope.

**Assumption.** The seed correction factor applies to the observed blank depletion;
the dichromate is in excess; no interference from chloride or nitrite.

**Check.** Do both validity tests: the depletion is $5.7\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$
(between 2 and 6) and the residual is $3.1\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ (above 1). For COD,
the blank consumed $24.60\ \text{cm}^{3}$, slightly less than the $25.00\ \text{cm}^{3}$
that would correspond to the full $10.00\ \text{cm}^{3}$ of dichromate, which is
consistent with a small reagent blank.

**Where marks are lost.** Forgetting the factor of $1.5$ between dichromate
equivalents and oxygen, which understates the COD by one third.

### C30 — Chloroform formation and the THM limit [Ch. 3] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Convert the fraction of DOC converted to moles of carbon, then to
moles of chloroform (one carbon per molecule), then to mass.

3.5\times0.0020 = 7.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{mg C dm}^{-3}

### C31 — Coagulant dose and membrane concentrate [Ch. 3] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Two separate mass balances: aluminium and alkalinity in the
coagulation step; nitrate and water in the membrane.

**Working.** (a)

$$
n_{\ce{Al2(SO4)3}} = \frac{28\times10^{-3}}{342.15} = 8.18\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}.

The same result follows from the mass fraction of aluminium in the salt,

\mathrm{Alk}_\text{consumed} = 2\times8.18\times10^{-5}\times3 = 4.91\times10^{-4}\ \text{eq dm}^{-3}
= 0.491\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}.
$$

(c) Water balance: permeate $= 0.75\times1000 = 750\ \text{m}^{3}\ \text{d}^{-1}$,

c_c = \frac{12\times1000 - 2.0\times750}{250} = \frac{12\,000-1500}{250}
= 42.0\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

Concentration factor: $42.0/12 = 3.5$, which equals $1/(1-0.75)$ — the rejection of
water, not the rejection of nitrate, sets the factor.

(d) The permeate is cleaner than the feed, so it is not the compliance problem. The
concentrate carries all the rejected nitrate, sulfate and hardness in one quarter of
the flow; it cannot be discharged to a small receiving water and must be treated,
reused or evaporated, and the disposal route sets the cost of the whole train.

**Answer.** (a) $4.42\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ Al, from $28\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ alum;
(b) $0.491\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$, $24.6\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ as $\ce{CaCO3}$, residual
$0.109\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$; (c) $42.0\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$, factor $3.5$;
(d) all rejected salt is concentrated in $25\ \%$ of the flow.

**Assumption.** Complete aluminium hydrolysis to $\ce{Al(OH)3}$; no nitrate
transformation in the membrane; recovery defined on a volume basis.

**Check.** The concentrate factor $1/(1-0.75) = 4$ is the maximum; the observed
$3.5$ is lower because $17\ \%$ of the nitrate passes into the permeate, which is
self-consistent.

**Where marks are lost.** Applying the concentration factor $4$ to the feed and
reporting $48\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$, which ignores the nitrate that passes the
membrane.

### C32 — Landfill gas from cellulose [Ch. 4] (5 marks)

**Strategy.** Balance the Buswell equation, convert the mass of substrate to moles,
and scale.

**Working.** (a) For $\ce{C6H10O5}$: $a=6$, $b=10$, $c=5$.

$$
\text{water} = a-\frac{b}{4}-\frac{c}{2} = 6-2.5-2.5 = 1.0,
$$

$$
\ce{CH4} = \frac{a}{2}+\frac{b}{8}-\frac{c}{4} = 3+1.25-1.25 = 3.0,
\qquad
\ce{CO2} = \frac{a}{2}-\frac{b}{8}+\frac{c}{4} = 3-1.25+1.25 = 3.0.
$$

$$
\ce{C6H10O5(s) + H2O(l) -> 3CH4(g) + 3CO2(g)}
$$

Atom balance: C $6=6$; H $10+2=12=3\times4$; O $5+1=6=3\times2$. Balanced.

(b) $n_\text{cellulose} = 1000/162.14 = 6.168\ \text{kmol}$, so
$n_{\ce{CH4}} = 3\times6.168 = 18.50\ \text{kmol}$.

$$
V_{\ce{CH4}} = 18.50\times10^{3}\times22.71\times10^{-3} = 420\ \text{m}^{3},
$$

$$
m_{\ce{CH4}} = 18.50\times16.041 = 297\ \text{kg}.
$$

(c) Fugitive fraction $= 0.40$: $m = 0.40\times297 = 119\ \text{kg}$.

$$
\mathrm{CO2\text{-}eq} = 0.119\ \text{t}\times28 = 3.32\ \text{t CO2\text{-}eq}.
$$

(d) Captured $= 0.60\times297 = 178\ \text{kg}$; slip $= 0.02\times178 =
3.56\ \text{kg}$;

$$
\mathrm{CO2\text{-}eq}_\text{slip} = 3.56\times10^{-3}\times28 = 0.0997\ \text{t}.
$$

Flaring destroys $98\ \%$ of the captured methane, so the energy-recovery route
reduces the methane term by roughly an order of magnitude relative to venting —
but it does not eliminate it, and the $0.1\ \text{t}$ slip is comparable with the
direct $\ce{CO2}$ from a small vehicle fleet over the same period.

**Answer.** (a) $\ce{C6H10O5 + H2O -> 3CH4 + 3CO2}$; (b) $4.2\times10^{2}\
\text{m}^{3}$, $297\ \text{kg}$; (c) $3.3\ \text{t CO2-eq}$; (d) $0.10\ \text{t
CO2-eq}$.

**Assumption.** Complete anaerobic conversion, no carbon to biomass or recalcitrant
residue, gas at 273.15 K and $10^{5}$ Pa, $\mathrm{GWP}_{100} = 28$ for methane.

**Check.** The gas is $50\ \%$ methane by volume, which is the lower end of the
$50$–$60\ \%$ range quoted for landfill gas; the mass of gas
($297+814 = 1111\ \text{kg}$) is slightly greater than the tonne of dry solids
because water is consumed — consistent with the $+1\ \ce{H2O}$ in the equation.

**Where marks are lost.** Using a water coefficient of $1.5$ and $\ce{CH4}$/$\ce{CO2}$
coefficients of $3.25$/$2.75$, which do not balance oxygen; the correct set is
$1.0$/$3.0$/$3.0$.

### D33 — The stratospheric ozone loss budget [Ch. 2] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** Compute each cycle's rate independently, sum, then test the
sensitivity to chlorine loading.

**Working.** (a) Dimer cycle (two ozone per dimer):

$$
2k[\ce{ClO}]^{2} = 2(2.0\times10^{-14})(1.0\times10^{10})^{2}
= 4.0\times10^{6}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

Single-chlorine cycle:

$$
k[\ce{Cl}][\ce{O3}] = (1.2\times10^{-11})(2.0\times10^{4})(4.0\times10^{12})
= 9.6\times10^{5}.
$$

$\ce{ClO + O}$ cycle:

$$
k[\ce{ClO}][\ce{O}] = (3.8\times10^{-11})(1.0\times10^{10})(1.0\times10^{6})
= 3.8\times10^{5}.
$$

(b) Total $= 5.34\times10^{6}\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

$$
t = \frac{4.0\times10^{12}}{5.34\times10^{6}} = 7.5\times10^{5}\ \text{s}
= 8.7\ \text{d}.
$$

(c) The $\ce{ClO + O}$ cycle needs atomic oxygen, whose concentration falls steeply
with decreasing altitude because ozone photolysis and $\ce{O2}$ photolysis both
weaken. In the lower stratosphere atomic oxygen is scarce, so the cycle that uses
two $\ce{ClO}$ molecules and no $\ce{O}$ takes over. Higher up, $[\ce{O}]$ is
sufficient and the $\ce{ClO + O}$ cycle is fast.

(d) A $30\ \%$ fall in chlorine gives $[\ce{ClO}] = 7.0\times10^{9}$:

$$
2(2.0\times10^{-14})(7.0\times10^{9})^{2} = 1.96\times10^{6}
\ \text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1},
$$

a fall of $51\ \%$. The loss is proportional to $[\ce{ClO}]^{2}$, so the response is
quadratic: a linear reduction in chlorine produces a much larger reduction in loss,
which is why the Montreal Protocol produced a faster recovery of the *rate* than of
the column.

(e) The dominant uncertainties are the rate constant for
$\ce{ClO + ClO + M}$ (strongly temperature- and pressure-dependent, and the step
that controls the whole cycle) and the assumed steady state for $\ce{Cl2O2}$ — its
photolysis rate and quantum yield are both uncertain, and the dimer also has a
thermal decomposition channel.

**Answer.** (a) $4.0\times10^{6}$, $9.6\times10^{5}$, $3.8\times10^{5}\
\text{molecules cm}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$; (b) $5.3\times10^{6}$, $8.7\ \text{d}$;
(c) atomic oxygen scarcity; (d) $1.96\times10^{6}$, $-51\ \%$; (e) $k(\ce{ClO+ClO+M})$
and the $\ce{Cl2O2}$ steady state.

**Assumption.** Steady state for the dimer; no transport; no heterogeneous chemistry
on aerosols; the quoted rate constants apply at 210 K.

**Check.** The dimer share ($75\ \%$) is consistent with C24, and the total
fractional loss ($11.5\ \%$ per day) is consistent with a springtime depletion
developing over weeks.

**Where marks are lost.** Comparing the three rates without the factor of two in the
dimer cycle, which makes the cycles appear to contribute almost equally.

### D34 — VOC-driven ozone production [Ch. 2] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** Follow the radicals: each $\ce{R}$-to-$\ce{RO2}$ conversion is one
$\ce{NO}$-to-$\ce{NO2}$ conversion, and each $\ce{NO2}$ is one ozone.

**Working.** (a) Counting the conversions per propene:

- $\ce{RO2 + NO}$: one $\ce{NO2}$;
- $\ce{HO2 + NO}$ after $\ce{RO + O2}$: one $\ce{NO2}$;
- formaldehyde, $\ce{HCHO ->[h\nu] HCO + H}$, gives $\ce{HCO + O2 -> HO2 + CO}$ and
  $\ce{H + O2 + M -> HO2 + M}$: two $\ce{HO2}$, hence two more $\ce{NO2}$.

Total $= 4\ \ce{NO2}$ per propene, hence $4\ \ce{O3}$ per propene, with the hydroxyl
radical regenerated so the chain continues.

(b) $10\ \text{ppb}$ of propene gives $4\times10 = 40\ \text{ppb}$ of ozone at
unlimited $\ce{NO}$.

(c) From C23 the Leighton baseline is $40.6\ \text{ppb}$ at
$[\ce{NO2}]/[\ce{NO}] = 3.0$. A measured $85\ \text{ppb}$ therefore leaves

$$
85-40.6 = 44.4\ \text{ppb}
$$

to be attributed to VOC chemistry, or $4.4\ \text{ppb}$ of ozone per ppb of propene.
This agrees with the mechanistic estimate of $4$ to within $10\ \%$, which is as
close as a one-VOC model can be expected to come.

(d) In a $\ce{NO_x}$-limited rural atmosphere the peroxy radicals cannot find
$\ce{NO}$ to convert; they instead react with each other or with $\ce{HO2}$, forming
peroxides and terminating the chain. Adding VOC then produces almost no ozone
because the limiting reagent is $\ce{NO_x}$, not the radical supply.

**Answer.** (a) $4\ \ce{NO2}$, $4\ \ce{O3}$ per propene; (b) $40\ \text{ppb}$;
(c) $44.4\ \text{ppb}$ attributed to VOC, $4.4\ \text{ppb}$ per ppb; (d) radical
termination without $\ce{NO}$.

**Assumption.** Unlimited $\ce{NO}$, no radical termination, unit ozone yield per
$\ce{NO2}$ photolysis, and no loss of ozone by titration; the mechanism is a
deliberate simplification.

**Check.** Four is the right order: measured incremental reactivities for propene
correspond to a few ozone molecules per molecule, never tens.

**Where marks are lost.** Counting the two fragments of formaldehyde photolysis as
a single $\ce{HO2}$ and obtaining $3$ instead of $4$.

### D35 — Methane, GWP and energy recovery [Ch. 2 and Ch. 4] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** Compare a pulse on two horizons, then net the combustion $\ce{CO2}$
against displaced grid power.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\mathrm{CO2\text{-}eq}_{20} = 1.0\times80 = 80\ \text{t},
\qquad
\mathrm{CO2\text{-}eq}_{100} = 1.0\times28 = 28\ \text{t}.
$$

(b) Energy $= 1000\ \text{kg}\times50.0\ \text{MJ kg}^{-1} = 5.00\times10^{4}\
\text{MJ} = 1.389\times10^{4}\ \text{kWh}$. Electrical output at $40\ \%$:
$5.556\times10^{3}\ \text{kWh}$. Avoided $\ce{CO2}$:

$$
5.556\times10^{3}\times0.35 = 1.944\times10^{3}\ \text{kg} = 1.94\ \text{t}.
$$

(c) Burning one tonne of methane releases

$$
\frac{44.01}{16.04}\times1.0 = 2.74\ \text{t CO2}.
$$

Net: $2.74-1.94 = 0.80\ \text{t CO2}$ emitted.

Reduction relative to venting over 100 years:

$$
\frac{28-0.80}{28}\times100 = 97\ \%.
$$

(d) Over 20 years methane is $80$ times $\ce{CO2}$ per tonne, over 100 years $28$
times; the ratio between the two gases therefore depends on the horizon, and a
short horizon makes methane look relatively worse. Coal-fired electricity emits
of the order of $0.9\ \text{kg CO2}\ \text{kWh}^{-1}$, so displacing it always avoids
far more $\ce{CO2}$ than the $2.74\ \text{t}$ released by combustion, whatever the
horizon: the comparison against coal is a mass balance, not a GWP-weighted one.

**Answer.** (a) $80\ \text{t}$ and $28\ \text{t CO2-eq}$; (b) $5.56\times10^{3}\
\text{kWh}$, $1.94\ \text{t CO2}$ avoided; (c) $0.80\ \text{t CO2}$ net, $97\ \%$
reduction; (d) horizon dependence and the coal comparison.

**Assumption.** Complete combustion, $100\ \%$ of the methane captured and burned, no
parasitic load, and a grid intensity that is constant over the year.

**Check.** The stoichiometric ratio $44.01/16.04 = 2.74$ is the standard
$\ce{CH4}$-to-$\ce{CO2}$ mass factor; and $0.80\ \text{t}$ is small compared with
$28\ \text{t}$, so the conclusion does not depend on the precise efficiency.

**Where marks are lost.** Adding the avoided emissions to the GWP instead of
subtracting the combustion $\ce{CO2}$ first and comparing in the same units.

### D36 — Retardation of a cadmium plume [Ch. 3] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** $R = 1 + (\rho_b/\theta)K_d$, then $v_c = v/R$.

**Working.** (a)

$$
R = 1+\frac{1.6}{0.35}\times45 = 1+205.7 = 206.7.
$$

(b)

$$
v_c = \frac{0.30}{206.7} = 1.45\times10^{-3}\ \text{m d}^{-1}
= 5.30\times10^{-1}\ \text{m yr}^{-1}.
$$

(c)

$$
t = \frac{150}{0.530} = 283\ \text{yr}.
$$

(d) With $K_d = 4.5$:

$$
R = 1+\frac{1.6}{0.35}\times4.5 = 21.6,
\qquad
v_c = \frac{0.30}{21.6}\times365 = 5.08\ \text{m yr}^{-1},
$$

$$
t = \frac{150}{5.08} = 29.5\ \text{yr}.
$$

A tenfold fall in $K_d$ shortens the travel time by a factor of about ten. The same
plume that was "contained" for three centuries becomes a thirty-year problem if the
aquifer acidifies.

(e) $K_d$ is an empirical lumped parameter that hides the chemistry: it depends on
pH, redox, ionic strength, competing cations and the nature of the sorbent, and it
is measured at one condition. A single value cannot describe a plume that crosses a
pH gradient. Cadmium mobility increases when the pH falls (less adsorption and less
hydrolysis), when chloride rises (formation of $\ce{CdCl+}$ and
$\ce{CdCl2(aq)}$), and when competing cations such as $\ce{Ca^{2+}}$ displace it
from exchange sites.

**Answer.** (a) $207$; (b) $0.530\ \text{m yr}^{-1}$; (c) $283\ \text{yr}$;
(d) $29.5\ \text{yr}$; (e) lumped parameter, pH- and ligand-dependent.

**Assumption.** Linear, reversible, equilibrium sorption; homogeneous aquifer; steady
one-dimensional flow; no degradation of the metal (correct, since metals do not
degrade).

**Check.** $R\approx1$ would give $0.30\ \text{m d}^{-1} = 110\ \text{m yr}^{-1}$;
a factor of $207$ slower is $0.53\ \text{m yr}^{-1}$, consistent with the computed
value.

**Where marks are lost.** Using porosity instead of bulk density, or inverting the
ratio, which is how the "contained for ever" conclusion is usually produced from a
wrong number.

### D37 — CT, disinfection and the THM trade-off [Ch. 3] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** Integrate the decaying chlorine concentration for CT, then invert for
the contact time and evaluate the by-product at both times.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\mathrm{CT_{eff}} = \frac{2.0}{0.010}\left(1-e^{-0.010\times60}\right)
= 200(1-0.5488) = 90.2\ \text{mg min dm}^{-3}.
$$

(b) Solve $150 = 200(1-e^{-0.010t})$:

$$
1-e^{-0.010t} = 0.75,
\qquad
t = \frac{-\ln(0.25)}{0.010} = 139\ \text{min}.
$$

(c) THM at 60 min:

$$
120(1-e^{-0.008\times60}) = 120(1-0.6188) = 45.7\ \mu\text{g dm}^{-3}.
$$

At 139 min:

$$
120(1-e^{-0.008\times139}) = 120(1-0.329) = 80.4\ \mu\text{g dm}^{-3}.
$$

The short contact time meets neither the CT target nor the THM limit; the longer
contact time meets the CT target but marginally exceeds the THM limit.

(d) Options: remove precursors before chlorination (enhanced coagulation, activated
carbon) — capital and operating cost; use chloramine as the primary disinfectant —
weaker against *Cryptosporidium* and can form nitrosamines; use UV or ozone as the
primary barrier and chlorine only for residual — no residual from UV or ozone, so a
secondary disinfectant is still needed; reduce pH to shift the equilibrium towards
HOCl — corrosion control then becomes mandatory.

**Answer.** (a) $90.2\ \text{mg min dm}^{-3}$; (b) $139\ \text{min}$;
(c) $45.7$ and $80.4\ \mu\text{g dm}^{-3}$; (d) four options with penalties.

**Assumption.** Pseudo-first-order chlorine decay with a constant $k$; a single
THM formation asymptote; CT measured as the integral, not the product of initial
concentration and time.

**Check.** The peak CT (no decay) is $2.0\times60 = 120\ \text{mg min dm}^{-3}$,
above the integrated value of $90.2$; the integrated value must be lower, and it is.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $C_0t$ instead of the integral, which overstates the
CT by $33\ \%$ and produces a false pass.

### D38 — PFAS removal by activated carbon [Ch. 3 and Ch. 4] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** Apply the Freundlich isotherm at the target concentration, then close
the mass balance on the batch.

**Working.** (a) At $C_e = 0.10\ \mu\text{g dm}^{-3}$:

$$
q = 45(0.10)^{0.40} = 45\times0.398 = 17.9\ \mu\text{g g}^{-1}.
$$

Mass balance per litre: $(C_0-C_e)/D = q$:

$$
D = \frac{0.50-0.10}{17.9} = 0.0223\ \text{g dm}^{-3} = 22.3\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

For $5000\ \text{m}^{3}\ \text{d}^{-1} = 5.0\times10^{6}\ \text{dm}^{3}$:

$$
m = 22.3\times5.0\times10^{6}\ \text{mg d}^{-1} = 112\ \text{kg d}^{-1}.
$$

(b)

$$
m_\text{PFOA} = (0.50-0.10)\times5.0\times10^{6}\ \mu\text{g}
= 2.0\times10^{6}\ \mu\text{g} = 2.0\ \text{g d}^{-1}.
$$

Loading $= 2.0\ \text{g}/112\ \text{kg} = 17.9\ \mu\text{g g}^{-1}$, which reproduces
$q$; the balance closes.

(c) At $0.004\ \mu\text{g dm}^{-3}$:

$$
q = 45(0.004)^{0.40} = 45\times1.10\times10^{-1} = 4.94\ \mu\text{g g}^{-1},
$$

$$
D = \frac{0.50-0.004}{4.94} = 0.100\ \text{g dm}^{-3} = 100\ \text{mg dm}^{-3},
$$

$$
m = 100\times5.0\times10^{6}\ \text{mg d}^{-1} = 5.0\times10^{2}\ \text{kg d}^{-1}.
$$

A $25$-fold tighter target costs $4.5$ times more carbon, because the isotherm is
concave: capacity falls as the equilibrium concentration falls, so the dose rises
more steeply than the target falls.

(d) Two grams of PFAS per day become $112\ \text{kg}$ of contaminated carbon per
day. The carbon does not destroy anything: the PFAS is transferred to a solid that
must be incinerated at above about $1000\ ^\circ\text{C}$, oxidised
hydrothermally, or consigned to a secure landfill. Each of those routes has its own
emissions and its own uncertainty, and incomplete destruction can produce volatile
fluorinated products. Adsorption is a concentration step, not a treatment endpoint.

**Answer.** (a) $17.9\ \mu\text{g g}^{-1}$, $22.3\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$, $112\ \text{kg d}^{-1}$;
(b) $2.0\ \text{g d}^{-1}$, $17.9\ \mu\text{g g}^{-1}$; (c) $4.94\ \mu\text{g g}^{-1}$,
$100\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$, $5.0\times10^{2}\ \text{kg d}^{-1}$; (d) transfer, not
destruction.

**Assumption.** Single-stage equilibrium contact, no competitive adsorption by
natural organic matter, isotherm valid over two orders of magnitude of
concentration.

**Check.** The loading computed in (b) equals the isotherm capacity used in (a);
if they differed, the mass balance would be wrong.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the influent concentration instead of the equilibrium
concentration in the isotherm, which overstates capacity and understates the dose.

### D39 — Recycling against incineration for PET [Ch. 4] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** Compare both options in the same units: tonnes of $\ce{CO2}$ avoided
or emitted per tonne of waste, plus primary energy.

**Working.** (a) Carbon fraction of the PET repeat unit:

$$
\frac{10\times12.011}{192.17} = 0.6250.
$$

$$
m_{\ce{CO2}} = 1.0\ \text{t}\times0.6250\times\frac{44.01}{12.011}
= 2.29\ \text{t CO2}.
$$

(b) Recycling avoids $0.75\ \text{t}$ of virgin PET:

$$
0.75\times2.3 = 1.725\ \text{t CO2-eq avoided},
$$

less the process emissions of $0.40\ \text{t}$:

$$
\text{net avoided} = 1.325\ \text{t CO2-eq}.
$$

(c) Electricity from incineration:

$$
1.0\ \text{t}\times23\ \text{GJ t}^{-1} = 23\ \text{GJ}
= 6.389\times10^{3}\ \text{kWh},
$$

$$
\text{net electrical} = 0.25\times6.389\times10^{3} = 1.597\times10^{3}\ \text{kWh},
$$

$$
\text{avoided} = 1.597\times10^{3}\times0.35 = 0.559\ \text{t CO2}.
$$

$$
\text{net emitted} = 2.29-0.559 = 1.73\ \text{t CO2}.
$$

(d)

$$
\Delta = 1.325+1.73 = 3.06\ \text{t CO2-eq per tonne of PET}.
$$

Primary energy: recycling saves $0.75\times70 = 52.5\ \text{GJ}$; incineration
recovers $23\ \text{GJ}$, of which $5.75\ \text{GJ}$ becomes electricity. On a
total-energy basis the saving is $52.5/23 = 2.3$ times the recovery; on an
electricity basis it is $52.5/5.75 = 9.1$ times. Both comparisons favour recycling,
by different margins.

Caveat: each mechanical cycle shortens the polymer chains, so the flake can only be
recycled into lower-grade products a limited number of times; the $75\ \%$ yield is
not indefinitely repeatable, and the displaced virgin material is a lower-grade
product each time.

**Answer.** (a) $2.29\ \text{t CO2}$; (b) $1.33\ \text{t}$ avoided; (c) $1.73\
\text{t}$ emitted; (d) $3.06\ \text{t}$ in favour of recycling; $52.5$ vs
$23\ \text{GJ}$.

**Assumption.** Virgin PET figures are cradle-to-gate; the electricity displaced is
at the stated grid intensity; the incinerator operates at its design efficiency.

**Check.** The carbon fraction $0.625$ matches the formula $\ce{C10H8O4}$
($120.1/192.17$) and the factor $44.01/12.011 = 3.664$ converts carbon to
$\ce{CO2}$; the arithmetic is therefore internally consistent.

**Where marks are lost.** Comparing $1.33\ \text{t}$ avoided with $2.29\ \text{t}$
released without subtracting the displaced electricity, which exaggerates the
advantage of recycling by about $60\ \%$.

### D40 — A well-mixed box model for urban NOₓ [Ch. 2] (7 marks)

**Strategy.** At steady state, concentration equals emission rate divided by the
first-order loss rate; the box volume converts a mass flux to a volumetric source.

**Working.** (a)

$$
V = 30\times10^{3}\times30\times10^{3}\times500 = 4.5\times10^{11}\ \text{m}^{3}.
$$

$$
E = \frac{120\times10^{6}\ \text{g d}^{-1}}{46.01\ \text{g mol}^{-1}}
= 2.608\times10^{6}\ \text{mol d}^{-1} = 30.19\ \text{mol s}^{-1},
$$

$$
\frac{E}{V} = \frac{30.19}{4.5\times10^{11}} = 6.71\times10^{-11}\
\text{mol m}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

(b) Lifetime $\tau = 12\ \text{h} = 4.32\times10^{4}\ \text{s}$, so
$k = 2.315\times10^{-5}\ \text{s}^{-1}$:

$$
c = \frac{E/V}{k} = \frac{6.71\times10^{-11}}{2.315\times10^{-5}}
= 2.898\times10^{-6}\ \text{mol m}^{-3},
$$

$$
\rho = 2.898\times10^{-6}\times46.01 = 1.333\times10^{-4}\ \text{g m}^{-3}
= 133\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3},
$$

$$
\chi = \frac{2.898\times10^{-6}}{40.87}\times10^{9} = 70.9\ \text{ppb}.
$$

(c) $133/40 = 3.3$, so the city exceeds the limit value by a factor of $3.3$. To
comply:

$$
\frac{133-40}{133} = 70.0\ \%,
$$

so emissions must fall by $70\ \%$ — from $120$ to $36\ \text{t d}^{-1}$.

(d) Assumptions: (i) the box is well mixed, so the concentration is uniform;
(ii) the emission is uniform over the box; (iii) the loss is first order with a
single lifetime, and the background inflow is negligible. For a city of this size
the dominant uncertainty is the **mixing height**, which varies diurnally by a
factor of three to five between the night-time inversion and the afternoon
convective boundary layer; the concentration is inversely proportional to it, so a
$300\ \text{m}$ night-time mixing height triples the predicted concentration.

**Answer.** (a) $4.5\times10^{11}\ \text{m}^{3}$,
$6.7\times10^{-11}\ \text{mol m}^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$; (b) $133\ \mu\text{g m}^{-3}$,
$71\ \text{ppb}$; (c) $3.3\times$ the limit, $70\ \%$ reduction; (d) mixing height.

**Assumption.** Steady state, uniform mixing, first-order loss, no advective inflow
of polluted air, $\ce{NO_x}$ treated as $\ce{NO2}$.

**Check.** $c = E\tau/V$: $2.608\times10^{6}\times0.5/4.5\times10^{11}$ is the same
$2.9\times10^{-6}\ \text{mol m}^{-3}$, so the two routes agree.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the mixing height in kilometres, which underestimates
the volume by $10^{3}$ and overstates the concentration by the same factor.

### E41 — Incineration stoichiometry and flue gas [Ch. 4] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** Convert the ultimate analysis to moles per kilogram, apply
$n_{\ce{O2}} = n_\ce{C} + n_\ce{H}/4 + n_\ce{S} - n_\ce{O}/2$, then add excess air and
build the dry flue gas from its components.

**Working.** (a) Moles per kilogram of waste:

| Element | Mass fraction | $M$ / g mol⁻¹ | $n$ / mol kg⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 0.360 | 12.011 | 29.97 |
| H | 0.048 | 1.008 | 47.62 |
| O | 0.220 | 15.999 | 13.75 |
| S | 0.005 | 32.06 | 0.1560 |
| N | 0.010 | 14.007 | 0.7139 |

$$
n_{\ce{O2}} = 29.97+\frac{47.62}{4}+0.1560-\frac{13.75}{2}
= 29.97+11.90+0.156-6.875 = 35.16\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}.
$$

$$
m_{\ce{O2}} = 35.16\times32.00\times10^{-3} = 1.125\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}.
$$

(b) Stoichiometric air:

$$
n_\text{air} = \frac{35.16}{0.210} = 167.4\ \text{mol kg}^{-1},
\qquad
m_\text{air} = 167.4\times28.97\times10^{-3} = 4.85\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}.
$$

At $50\ \%$ excess: $251.1\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$ and $7.28\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$.

(c) Oxygen supplied $= 0.210\times251.1 = 52.74\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$; excess
$= 52.74-35.16 = 17.58$.

Nitrogen from air $= 0.790\times251.1 = 198.4$; from fuel $= 0.7139/2 = 0.357$;
total $= 198.8$.

Products per kilogram: $\ce{CO2}$ $29.97$; $\ce{SO2}$ $0.156$; $\ce{N2}$ $198.8$;
$\ce{O2}$ $17.58$. Dry total $= 246.5\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$.

$$
\text{CO}_2:\ \frac{29.97}{246.5} = 12.16\ \%;
\qquad
\text{O}_2:\ \frac{17.58}{246.5} = 7.13\ \%;
\qquad
\text{N}_2:\ \frac{198.8}{246.5} = 80.65\ \%.
$$

$$
\text{SO}_2:\ \frac{0.156}{246.5} = 6.33\times10^{-4} = 633\ \text{ppm}.
$$

Dry gas volume at 273.15 K and $10^{5}$ Pa:

$$
V = 246.5\times22.71\times10^{-3} = 5.60\ \text{m}^{3}\ \text{kg}^{-1}.
$$

$$
\rho_{\ce{SO2}} = \frac{0.156\times64.07}{5.60} = 1.785\ \text{g m}^{-3}
= 1785\ \text{mg m}^{-3}.
$$

(d) $1785\ \text{mg m}^{-3}$ is about thirty-six times the $50\ \text{mg m}^{-3}$
limit. Uncontrolled combustion cannot comply; a scrubber is mandatory, and the
stoichiometric lime demand follows from the $\ce{SO2}$ flow (compare E47).

**Answer.** (a) $35.2\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$, $1.13\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$;
(b) $167.4$ and $251.1\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}$ ($4.85$ and $7.28\ \text{kg kg}^{-1}$);
(c) $\ce{CO2}$ $12.2\ \%$, $\ce{O2}$ $7.1\ \%$, $\ce{N2}$ $80.7\ \%$,
$\ce{SO2}$ $633\ \text{ppm}$ or $1.8\times10^{3}\ \text{mg m}^{-3}$;
(d) no.

**Assumption.** Complete combustion with no $\ce{CO}$ or unburned carbon; all sulfur
to $\ce{SO2}$; fuel nitrogen to $\ce{N2}$; ideal gas at the reference conditions;
ash and moisture inert apart from the water vapour, which is excluded from the dry
basis.

**Check.** The mole fractions sum to $12.16+7.13+80.65+0.06 = 100.0\ \%$. The flue
gas $\ce{O2}$ of $7.1\ \%$ corresponds to roughly $50\ \%$ excess air, which is
consistent with the assumption rather than with the calculation — a useful
cross-check that the excess air was applied once, not twice.

**Where marks are lost.** Subtracting the fuel oxygen as $n_\ce{O}$ instead of
$n_\ce{O}/2$, which overstates the oxygen demand and the air flow.

### E42 — Two-endpoint alkalinity titration [Ch. 3] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** Compare the two titres: the phenolphthalein titre measures hydroxide
plus half the carbonate; the methyl orange titre measures total alkalinity.

**Working.** (a) $P = 3.20\ \text{cm}^{3}$, $T = 12.80\ \text{cm}^{3}$. Since
$P < T/2$, there is no hydroxide; the sample contains carbonate and bicarbonate.

(b) The phenolphthalein endpoint converts carbonate to bicarbonate, consuming one
equivalent per carbonate:

$$
[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] = \frac{0.0200\times3.20\times10^{-3}}{50.00\times10^{-3}}
= 1.28\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3} = 1.28\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}.
$$

Total alkalinity from the full titre:

$$
\mathrm{Alk} = \frac{0.0200\times12.80\times10^{-3}}{50.00\times10^{-3}}
= 5.12\times10^{-3}\ \text{eq dm}^{-3} = 5.12\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}.
$$

$$
[\ce{HCO3^-}] = \mathrm{Alk}-2[\ce{CO3^{2-}}]
= 5.12-2(1.28) = 2.56\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}.
$$

(c) Alkalinity as $\ce{CaCO3}$:

$$
5.12\times50.04 = 256\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

(d) Phosphate, silicate and borate are also titrated in the same pH range and
consume acid without being carbonate; they inflate the apparent alkalinity. Organic
acids behave similarly near pH 4.5. A carbonate-speciation calculation from the
titres alone is therefore valid only when these are negligible or are measured
separately.

**Answer.** (a) carbonate plus bicarbonate, no hydroxide; (b) $[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] =
1.28\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}$, $[\ce{HCO3^-}] = 2.56\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}$;
(c) $5.12\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$, $256\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ as $\ce{CaCO3}$;
(d) non-carbonate buffers.

**Assumption.** Strong-acid titration with a sharp endpoint; activity coefficients
unity; no other weak acids.

**Check.** $2[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] + [\ce{HCO3^-}] = 2(1.28)+2.56 = 5.12\
\text{meq dm}^{-3}$, reproducing the total alkalinity exactly.

**Where marks are lost.** Taking $[\ce{HCO3^-}] = T-P$ in millilitres and forgetting
that the second stage also titrates the bicarbonate formed from carbonate.

### E43 — Closed and open sodium bicarbonate solutions [Ch. 3] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** In the sealed vessel the sodium concentration is fixed by the
dissolution and must be balanced by carbonate species; on opening, the alkalinity is
conserved but the carbon content is set by $p_{\ce{CO2}}$.

**Working.** (a) For $\ce{NaHCO3}$ dissolved to $C_T = 3.0\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}$,
$[\ce{Na+}] = 3.0\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$. Charge balance:

$$
[\ce{Na+}] + [\ce{H+}] = [\ce{HCO3^-}] + 2[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] + [\ce{OH^-}].
$$

Solving numerically with $K_{a1} = 4.3\times10^{-7}$ and
$K_{a2} = 4.7\times10^{-11}$ gives $[\ce{H+}] = 4.7\times10^{-9}$ and

$$
\mathrm{pH} = 8.33.
$$

(b) At pH 8.33, $\alpha_1 = 0.980$ and $\alpha_2 = 0.00989$; the carbonate fraction
is $0.99\ \%$, so $[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] = 3.0\times0.00989 = 29.7\ \mu\text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

(c) On opening, the alkalinity stays at $3.0\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$ and
$p_{\ce{CO2}} = 4.20\times10^{-4}\ \text{atm}$, so
$[\ce{H2CO3^*}] = K_Hp = 3.4\times10^{-2}\times4.20\times10^{-4}
= 1.43\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$.

Solving $\mathrm{Alk} = [\ce{HCO3^-}]\,\left(1+2K_{a2}/[\ce{H+}]\right)$ with
$[\ce{HCO3^-}] = K_{a1}[\ce{H2CO3^*}]/[\ce{H+}]$ gives
$[\ce{H+}] = 2.13\times10^{-9}$:

$$
\mathrm{pH} = 8.67,
$$

$$
[\ce{HCO3^-}] = 2.87\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3},\quad
[\ce{CO3^{2-}}] = 63\ \mu\text{mol dm}^{-3},
$$

$$
C_T = 1.43\times10^{-5}+2.873\times10^{-3}+6.3\times10^{-5}
= 2.95\ \text{mmol dm}^{-3}.
$$

(d) The pH moves by only $0.34$ units because the solution is buffered: the
alkalinity is fixed, and at pH near 8.5 the dominant species is bicarbonate, whose
concentration is set by the alkalinity rather than by the gas phase. Opening the
vessel changes the carbon inventory by only $1.6\ \%$; the buffer capacity, not the
gas exchange, controls the pH.

**Answer.** (a) pH 8.33; (b) $0.99\ \%$; (c) pH 8.67, $C_T = 2.95\
\text{mmol dm}^{-3}$; (d) bicarbonate buffering at fixed alkalinity.

**Assumption.** Closed system at (a) and (b); activities equal concentrations;
$25\ ^\circ\text{C}$; no other weak acids; equilibrium with the gas phase at (c).

**Check.** For a pure bicarbonate solution,
$\mathrm{pH}\approx(\mathrm{p}K_{a1}+\mathrm{p}K_{a2})/2 = (6.37+10.33)/2 = 8.35$,
which agrees with the numerical $8.33$ to within the approximation.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the familiar $\sqrt{K_HK_{a1}p_{\ce{CO2}}}$ formula
for (c), which is valid only for pure water with no alkalinity and gives pH 5.4.

### E44 — The Streeter–Phelps oxygen sag [Ch. 3] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** Differentiate the deficit expression to find the critical time, then
substitute to find the maximum deficit.

**Working.** (a) Setting $\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}t = 0$ gives

$$
t_c = \frac{\ln(k_2/k_1)}{k_2-k_1} = \frac{\ln 2}{0.30} = 2.31\ \text{d}.
$$

(b)

$$
D_\text{max} = \frac{0.30\times30}{0.30}\left(e^{-0.30\times2.31}
- e^{-0.60\times2.31}\right) + 1.0\,e^{-0.60\times2.31}
$$

$$
= 30(0.500-0.250)+0.250 = 7.50+0.25 = 7.75\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

$$
\mathrm{DO}_\text{min} = 9.1-7.75 = 1.35\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}.
$$

(c) $1.35\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ is far below the $5.0\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$ criterion.
The maximum permitted deficit is $9.1-5.0 = 4.10\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$. At the
critical time the initial-deficit term contributes $0.25$, so the load term may be
at most $4.10-0.25 = 3.85\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$. Because that term is proportional to
$L_0$:

$$
L_0' = 30\times\frac{3.85}{7.50} = 15.4\ \text{mg dm}^{-3},
$$

a reduction of $\dfrac{30-15.4}{30} = 49\ \%$.

(d) The critical time depends on $k_1$ and $k_2$ but not on $L_0$, so the assumption
holds exactly within the model; it fails because a treatment that reduces $L_0$ by
half also usually reduces $k_1$ (the residual substrate is less readily
biodegradable), which moves the sag downstream and can deepen it slightly.

**Answer.** (a) $2.31\ \text{d}$; (b) $D_\text{max} = 7.75\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$,
$\mathrm{DO}_\text{min} = 1.35\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$; (c) $49\ \%$ reduction, to
$L_0' = 15.4\ \text{mg dm}^{-3}$; (d) $k_1$ also changes.

**Assumption.** Plug flow with no dispersion, constant $k_1$ and $k_2$, constant
temperature and saturation, and a single instantaneous discharge.

**Check.** At $t = 2.31\ \text{d}$ the deficit is larger than at $1\ \text{d}$
($6.31$) and at $5\ \text{d}$ ($5.25$); the curve has a single maximum, as the model
requires.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $L_0 = 30$ with $k_2 < k_1$, which gives a negative
argument and a non-physical deficit.

### E45 — Green metrics for aspirin [Ch. 1] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** AE from the equation; yield from the limiting reagent; E, PMI and RME
from the charged masses.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\mathrm{AE} = \frac{180.16}{138.12+102.09}\times100 = \frac{180.16}{240.21}\times100
= 75.0\ \%.
$$

(b) $n_\text{salicylic} = 50.0/138.12 = 0.3620\ \text{kmol}$;
$n_\text{anhydride} = 40.0/102.09 = 0.3918\ \text{kmol}$. The salicylic acid is

m_\text{theoretical} = 0.3620\times180.16 = 65.2\ \text{kg},
\qquad
Y = \frac{55.0}{65.2}\times100 = 84.3\ \%.

(c) Total input $= 50.0+40.0+250+150 = 490\ \text{kg}$; waste

### E46 — Radon in a ventilated basement [Ch. 2] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** A one-box steady state with a source, radioactive decay and
ventilation: $C = S/(\lambda + a)$.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{0.6931}{3.82\times86\,400}
= 2.10\times10^{-6}\ \text{s}^{-1}.
$$

(b) Ventilation $a = 0.50/3600 = 1.389\times10^{-4}\ \text{s}^{-1}$:

$$
C = \frac{5.0\times10^{-3}}{2.10\times10^{-6}+1.389\times10^{-4}}
= \frac{5.0\times10^{-3}}{1.410\times10^{-4}} = 35.5\ \text{Bq m}^{-3}.
$$

(c) At $0.25$ air changes per hour, $a = 6.944\times10^{-5}$:

$$
C = \frac{5.0\times10^{-3}}{7.154\times10^{-5}} = 69.9\ \text{Bq m}^{-3}.
$$

(d) For $C = 50\ \text{Bq m}^{-3}$:

$$
a = \frac{S}{C}-\lambda = \frac{5.0\times10^{-3}}{50}-2.10\times10^{-6}
= 9.79\times10^{-5}\ \text{s}^{-1} = 0.35\ \text{h}^{-1}.
$$

(e) Room volume $= 10.0\times8.0\times2.4 = 192\ \text{m}^{3}$; total activity
$= 35.5\times192 = 6.82\times10^{3}\ \text{Bq}$.

$$
N = \frac{A}{\lambda} = \frac{6.82\times10^{3}}{2.10\times10^{-6}}
= 3.25\times10^{9}\ \text{atoms},
$$

$$
m = \frac{3.25\times10^{9}}{6.022\times10^{23}}\times222
= 1.2\times10^{-12}\ \text{g} = 1.2\ \text{pg}.
$$

**Answer.** (a) $2.10\times10^{-6}\ \text{s}^{-1}$; (b) $35.5\ \text{Bq m}^{-3}$;
(c) $69.9\ \text{Bq m}^{-3}$; (d) $0.35\ \text{h}^{-1}$; (e) $1.2\ \text{pg}$.

**Assumption.** Well-mixed room, constant source, no plate-out or aerosol
attachment, outdoor radon negligible, steady state.

**Check.** Decay removes only
$2.1\times10^{-6}/(1.41\times10^{-4}) = 1.5\ \%$ of the radon; ventilation removes the
rest. Halving the ventilation roughly doubles the concentration ($35.5 \to 69.9$),
as $C \propto 1/(\lambda+a)$ requires when $\lambda \ll a$.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the half-life in days directly in a rate equation
with time in seconds, which produces a decay constant a thousand times too large.

### E47 — Lime scrubbing of SO₂ and HCl [Ch. 4] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** Convert concentrations and flow to mass flows, then to moles; apply
one lime per $\ce{SO2}$ and one half per $\ce{HCl}$.

**Working.** (a)

$$
\dot m_{\ce{SO2}} = 800\times10^{-6}\times1.00\times10^{5}
= 80.0\ \text{kg h}^{-1},
\qquad
\dot m_{\ce{HCl}} = 400\times10^{-6}\times1.00\times10^{5}
= 40.0\ \text{kg h}^{-1}.
$$

(b)

$$
\dot n_{\ce{SO2}} = \frac{80.0}{64.07} = 1.249\ \text{kmol h}^{-1},
\qquad
\dot n_{\ce{HCl}} = \frac{40.0}{36.46} = 1.097\ \text{kmol h}^{-1}.
$$

$$
\dot n_{\ce{Ca(OH)2}} = 1.249+\frac{1.097}{2} = 1.249+0.549 = 1.797\ \text{kmol h}^{-1},
$$

$$
\dot m = 1.797\times74.09 = 133\ \text{kg h}^{-1};
\qquad
\text{with } 20\ \%\ \text{excess}: 160\ \text{kg h}^{-1}.
$$

(c) Residue: $\ce{CaSO3}$ $1.249\times120.17 = 150\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$;
$\ce{CaCl2}$ $0.549\times110.98 = 60.9\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$; unreacted lime
$0.20\times133 = 26.6\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$. Total $= 238\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$.

(d) With full oxidation to $\ce{CaSO4}$:
$1.249\times136.14 = 170\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$ in place of $150$, so the residue rises
to $170+60.9+26.6 = 257\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$, a $8\ \%$ increase. The oxidation matters
because sulfite is soluble and chemically reducing, whereas sulfate is stable and
less leachable; but the extra mass and the change in leaching behaviour both affect
the landfill classification of the residue.

**Answer.** (a) $80.0$ and $40.0\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$; (b) $1.80\ \text{kmol h}^{-1}$,
$133\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$, $160\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$ with excess; (c) $238\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$;
(d) $257\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$.

**Assumption.** All acid gas contacts the sorbent and reacts to completion; the lime
is $100\ \%$ $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$; the $20\ \%$ excess reports entirely to the residue;
fly ash is not included.

**Check.** Close the mass balance. Input: lime $160$ + $\ce{SO2}$ $80.0$ +
$\ce{HCl}$ $40.0 = 280\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$. Output: solids $238\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$
plus the water released by the two reactions,
$(1.249+2\times0.549)\times18.02 = 42.3\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$, giving
$280\ \text{kg h}^{-1}$. The balance closes.

**Where marks are lost.** Using one lime per $\ce{HCl}$ instead of one per two
$\ce{HCl}$, which doubles the lime demand for that gas.

### E48 — Chromium(VI) reduction and sludge [Ch. 4] (9 marks)

**Strategy.** Three electrons per chromium, so three iron(II) per chromium; then
precipitate both metals as hydroxides.

**Working.** (a) Mass of chromium:

$$
m_{\ce{Cr}} = 25\ \text{m}^{3}\times80\ \text{g m}^{-3} = 2000\ \text{g} = 2.00\ \text{kg},
$$

$$
n_{\ce{Cr(VI)}} = \frac{2000}{52.00} = 38.5\ \text{mol}.
$$

From $\ce{Cr2O7^{2-} + 6Fe^{2+} + 14H+ -> 2Cr^{3+} + 6Fe^{3+} + 7H2O}$, six
iron(II) reduce two chromium(VI), so three iron(II) per chromium(VI):

$$
n_{\ce{Fe}} = 3\times38.5 = 115\ \text{mol},
\qquad
m_{\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}} = 115\times278.01 = 32.1\ \text{kg}.
$$

(b) With $15\ \%$ excess: $32.1\times1.15 = 36.9\ \text{kg}$.

(c)

$$
m_{\ce{Cr(OH)3}} = 38.5\times103.02 = 3.96\ \text{kg},
\qquad
m_{\ce{Fe(OH)3}} = 115\times106.87 = 12.3\ \text{kg}.
$$

Total dry sludge $= 16.3\ \text{kg}$, or $16.3/25 = 0.65\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$.

(d) Sulfate from the ferrous sulfate is $115\ \text{mol}$; precipitated with lime as
$\ce{CaSO4}$:

$$
m_{\ce{CaSO4}} = 115\times172.17 = 19.9\ \text{kg}.
$$

Total $= 16.3+19.9 = 36.2\ \text{kg}$, or $1.45\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$ — more than
double the hydroxide-only figure. The sludge is hazardous because it contains
chromium(III), which can be re-oxidised to chromium(VI) under alkaline, oxidising
conditions; it must go to a licensed facility, and the gypsum content affects
leaching tests and the choice between landfill and recovery.

**Answer.** (a) $38.5\ \text{mol}$, $32.1\ \text{kg}$; (b) $36.9\ \text{kg}$;
(c) $3.96\ \text{kg}$ $\ce{Cr(OH)3}$, $12.3\ \text{kg}$ $\ce{Fe(OH)3}$,
$0.65\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$ dry; (d) $19.9\ \text{kg}$ gypsum, $36.2\ \text{kg}$ total.

**Assumption.** Complete reduction and complete precipitation; all iron(III)
precipitates as hydroxide; no other metals in the stream; sludge reported as dry
solids.

**Check.** The electron balance: 38.5 mol Cr(VI) accepts $3\times38.5 = 115\
\text{mol e}^-$, and 115 mol Fe(II) donates 115 mol e⁻. The redox is balanced.

**Where marks are lost.** Using a 1:1 iron-to-chromium ratio, which leaves two
thirds of the chromium unreduced.

## Part F — Multiple-choice answers

### F1 — Answer: B

Atom economy is computed from the balanced equation and the molar masses alone; it
contains no yield, no solvent and no catalyst. The best distractor is **C**: a
$100\ \%$ atom economy says nothing about solvent, work-up or purification, and a
perfectly atom-economical reaction run in thirty volumes of dichloromethane can have
an E-factor above $40$. Option A is wrong because yield and solvent recovery enter
RME and E-factor, not AE; D is wrong because catalysts and auxiliaries are outside
the AE boundary.

### F2 — Answer: B

Adding the three steps gives $\ce{2O3 -> 3O2}$: the chlorine cancels, one dimer
photolysis releases two chlorine atoms, and each destroys one ozone. The best
distractor is **A**, which is the net reaction of the *single-chlorine* cycle, not
of the dimer cycle. C is one step of a different cycle, and D is a single step of
this cycle, not the net reaction.

### F3 — Answer: B

In a VOC-limited regime ozone production is controlled by radical supply, but ozone
is destroyed by $\ce{NO + O3 -> NO2 + O2}$. Cutting $\ce{NO_x}$ reduces the titration
term, so ozone rises — the regime shift behind the weekend effect. The best
distractor is **A**, the intuitive but wrong "less precursor, less ozone" answer,
which holds only in the NOx-limited regime. C is wrong because VOC limitation does
not fix the ozone concentration; D describes the correct action, not the effect of
reducing $\ce{NO_x}$ alone.

### F4 — Answer: C

Alkalinity is capacity, not intensity. At pH 7.0 with only
$0.50\ \text{meq dm}^{-3}$ of alkalinity, a small acid addition shifts the pH
sharply, so the water is poorly buffered regardless of its neutral pH. The best
distractor is **A**: neutral pH is often mistaken for buffering, but buffering
requires a reserve of weak-acid anions. B confuses alkalinity with hardness, and D
has no basis — hardness and alkalinity are independent quantities that happen to
share a reporting unit.

### F5 — Answer: B

De novo synthesis is a surface-catalysed reaction between carbon in fly ash,
chlorine and oxygen over copper or iron, fastest between $250$ and
$450\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The engineering response is to shorten the residence time in
that window by rapid quenching and to remove the fly ash before it can catalyse
further formation. The best distractor is **A**: a high furnace temperature is
necessary for good combustion but does not prevent re-formation in the cooler
downstream train, which is where most de novo synthesis occurs. C and D increase
formation or are irrelevant.

### F6 — Answer: B

Granular activated carbon adsorbs PFAS; it does not break the C–F bond. The spent
carbon is a concentrated hazardous waste that must be incinerated at high
temperature, oxidised hydrothermally or landfilled in a secure cell. The best
distractor is **A**, which describes mineralisation — the outcome that adsorption

## Top ten errors in this level

| # | Error | Why it is wrong | Correct approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reporting atom economy as "greenness" | It ignores yield, solvent, energy and hazard | Report AE with yield, PMI and E-factor |
| 2 | Comparing E-factors with different boundaries | The metric is defined by its boundary | State the boundary and the solvent recovery rate |
