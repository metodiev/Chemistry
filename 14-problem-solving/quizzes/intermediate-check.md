# Intermediate Check

> **Level 2 · 20 questions · 25 minutes**
> **Covers:** chapters 1–13 of [Level 2 — Intermediate Chemistry](../../03-intermediate-chemistry/README.md).
> **Use:** at the end of Level 2, or as a diagnostic before starting Level 3.

Several items here are numerical, and a wrong answer often comes from one slipped
conversion rather than from a missing idea. Show your working on paper: the explanation
tells you which step the attractive distractor represents.

---

**Q1.** How many molecules are there in 0.50 mol of carbon dioxide?

- A. $3.01 \times 10^{23}$
- B. $6.02 \times 10^{23}$
- C. $1.20 \times 10^{24}$
- D. $3.01 \times 10^{22}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** $N = nN_A = 0.50 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}$. Option B is the count in one mole, which answers a different question; option D divides by 100 instead of by 2.

</details>

---

**Q2.** What is the mass of 0.25 mol of calcium carbonate, $M = 100.1\ \mathrm{g\ mol^{-1}}$?

- A. 25.0 g
- B. 50.0 g
- C. 100.1 g
- D. 12.5 g

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** $m = nM = 0.25 \times 100.1 = 25.0$ g. Option C is the molar mass itself, the mass of one mole rather than of a quarter of one; option B doubles instead of halving.

</details>

---

**Q3.** Hydrogen reacts with oxygen: $\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}$. If 4.0 mol of $\ce{H2}$ is mixed with 1.0 mol of $\ce{O2}$, which reactant limits the reaction and how much water forms?

- A. $\ce{O2}$ limits; 2.0 mol of water
- B. $\ce{H2}$ limits; 4.0 mol of water
- C. $\ce{O2}$ limits; 1.0 mol of water
- D. Neither limits; 4.0 mol of water

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** One mole of oxygen needs two moles of hydrogen and four are available, so hydrogen is in excess and oxygen is the limiting reactant. The product is therefore set by the oxygen: 1.0 mol $\ce{O2}$ gives 2.0 mol $\ce{H2O}$. Option B is the attractive distractor: it assumes the reactant present in the larger amount must limit, which ignores the 2:1 stoichiometry.

</details>

**Q4.** A reaction has a theoretical yield of 10.0 g and an actual yield of 8.0 g. What is the percentage yield?

- A. 80 per cent
- B. 125 per cent
- C. 20 per cent
- D. 8 per cent

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Percentage yield is actual divided by theoretical, times 100: $(8.0/10.0) \times 100 = 80$ per cent. Option B inverts the fraction, which would make the yield exceed the theoretical maximum.

</details>

**Q5.** 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ sodium chloride solution is diluted to a final volume of 250 cm³. What is the new concentration?

- A. 0.0200 mol dm⁻³
- B. 0.200 mol dm⁻³
- C. 2.00 mol dm⁻³
- D. 0.00200 mol dm⁻³

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** The amount of solute is unchanged, so $c_1V_1 = c_2V_2$ gives $c_2 = 0.200 \times 25.0/250 = 0.0200\ \mathrm{mol\ dm^{-3}}$. Option B is the common error of assuming dilution does not change the concentration; option C multiplies instead of dividing.

</details>

**Q6.** What is the volume occupied by 1.00 mol of an ideal gas at 0 °C and 100 kPa?

- A. 22.7 dm³
- B. 22.4 dm³
- C. 24.0 dm³
- D. 11.2 dm³

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** $V = nRT/p = (1.00 \times 8.314 \times 273.15)/100\,000 = 0.0227\ \mathrm{m^3} = 22.7$ dm³. Option B is the molar volume at 1 atm rather than 100 kPa; option D is the volume of half a mole.

</details>

**Q7.** Which intermolecular force is strongest in liquid hydrogen fluoride?

- A. Hydrogen bonding
- B. Dipole–dipole attraction
- C. London dispersion
- D. Ionic bonding

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** The very polar H–F bond, with hydrogen attached to a small electronegative atom bearing lone pairs, supports hydrogen bonding, which is stronger than the ordinary dipole–dipole attraction it is sometimes confused with. Option B is the attractive distractor because HF is polar, but dipole–dipole forces describe the weaker, non-directional part of the interaction. Option D is a category error: liquid HF is molecular, not ionic.

</details>

**Q8.** What is the shape of the water molecule?

- A. Linear, 180°
- B. Bent, about 104.5°
- C. Trigonal planar, 120°
- D. Tetrahedral, 109.5°

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Oxygen has four electron domains, two bonding pairs and two lone pairs, so the electron-pair geometry is tetrahedral and the molecular geometry is bent with an angle compressed below 109.5°. Option D describes the arrangement of the four domains, not of the atoms.

</details>

**Q9.** Carbon dioxide has two polar C=O bonds but no net dipole moment. Why?

- A. The C=O bonds are actually non-polar
- B. The molecule is linear, so the two bond dipoles are equal and opposite and cancel
- C. The oxygen atoms carry no partial charge
- D. The molecule is bent, so the dipoles cancel

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Each C=O bond is polar, but the 180° geometry makes the two dipoles equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so the vector sum is zero. Option A is the common misconception that a bond with two identical atoms is required for a molecule to be non-polar; option D reverses the geometry.

</details>

---

**Q10.** What is the hybridisation of each carbon atom in ethene, $\ce{C2H4}$?

- A. sp³
- B. sp²
- C. sp
- D. sp³d

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Each carbon has three σ bonds (two to hydrogen, one to the other carbon) and one π bond, which requires three hybrid orbitals at 120° and an unhybridised p orbital. Option A is the alkane case; option C would give a linear arrangement with two π bonds.

</details>

**Q11.** How much energy is needed to raise the temperature of 100 g of water by 10.0 K? Take $c = 4.18\ \mathrm{J\ g^{-1}\ K^{-1}}$.

- A. 4.18 kJ
- B. 41.8 kJ
- C. 0.418 kJ
- D. 418 kJ

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** $q = mc\Delta T = 100 \times 4.18 \times 10.0 = 4180\ \mathrm{J} = 4.18$ kJ. Option B multiplies by an extra factor of ten, and option D fails to convert joules to kilojoules.

</details>

**Q12.** For a reaction A → B, $\Delta H = -50\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$; for B → C, $\Delta H = +20\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$. What is $\Delta H$ for A → C?

- A. $-30\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$
- B. $+30\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$
- C. $-70\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$
- D. $+70\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Enthalpy is a state function, so the two steps add: $-50 + 20 = -30\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}$. Option C subtracts the magnitudes instead of adding the signed values, which would require the second step to be exothermic as well.

</details>

**Q13.** Which statement about a catalyst is correct?

- A. It lowers the activation energy and leaves $\Delta H$ unchanged
- B. It lowers the activation energy and makes $\Delta H$ more negative
- C. It increases the activation energy but increases the yield
- D. It increases the equilibrium constant

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower barrier; it does not change the energies of the reactants or products. Option B is the most common misconception: a faster reaction is not a more exothermic one, and the position of the equilibrium is unaffected.

</details>

---

**Q14.** For $\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}$, what happens when the total pressure is increased at constant temperature?

- A. The equilibrium shifts to the right, towards ammonia
- B. The equilibrium shifts to the left
- C. There is no change
- D. The equilibrium constant increases

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Four moles of gas become two, so raising the pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules. Option D confuses the shift with a change in $K$: the equilibrium constant depends only on temperature, and the shift occurs because the reaction quotient is displaced from it.

</details>

**Q15.** Two solutions have the same concentration, 0.10 mol dm⁻³: one of hydrochloric acid and one of ethanoic acid. Which has the higher pH?

- A. The ethanoic acid solution
- B. The hydrochloric acid solution
- C. They are equal
- D. It depends on the volume of each solution

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Ethanoic acid is weak and only partly dissociated, so its hydrogen ion concentration is lower and its pH is higher. Option C is the attractive distractor: equal concentration does not mean equal hydrogen ion concentration, because the extent of dissociation differs.

</details>

---

**Q16.** A buffer solution is one that:

- A. resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added
- B. has a pH of exactly 7
- C. cannot be diluted
- D. contains only a strong acid and its salt

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** A buffer contains a weak acid and its conjugate base, or a weak base and its conjugate acid, and neutralises small additions of either. Option B confuses neutrality with buffering; a buffer can be acidic, neutral or alkaline.

</details>

**Q17.** What is the oxidation number of manganese in $\ce{KMnO4}$?

- A. $+7$
- B. $+5$
- C. $+4$
- D. $-1$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Potassium is $+1$ and each oxygen is $-2$, so $+1 + x + 4(-2) = 0$ gives $x = +7$. Option B is the oxidation state of manganese in manganate(V), $\ce{MnO4^{3-}}$, a different ion; option C is manganese in $\ce{MnO2}$.

</details>

**Q18.** A cell is made from a zinc electrode in $\ce{Zn^2+}$ and a copper electrode in $\ce{Cu^2+}$. Given $E^\circ(\ce{Zn^2+}/\ce{Zn}) = -0.76$ V and $E^\circ(\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{Cu}) = +0.34$ V, what is $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$?

- A. $+1.10$ V
- B. $-1.10$ V
- C. $+0.42$ V
- D. $+0.76$ V

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** $E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10$ V. Option C adds the two values with the wrong signs, and option B reverses the cell and would describe a non-spontaneous process.

</details>

**Q19.** The solubility product of silver chloride is $1.8 \times 10^{-10}$. What is its molar solubility in pure water?

- A. $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$ mol dm⁻³
- B. $1.8 \times 10^{-10}$ mol dm⁻³
- C. $9.0 \times 10^{-11}$ mol dm⁻³
- D. $1.3 \times 10^{-10}$ mol dm⁻³

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** For a 1:1 salt, $K_{sp} = s^2$, so $s = \sqrt{1.8 \times 10^{-10}} = 1.3 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\ dm^{-3}}$. Option B copies $K_{sp}$ as though it were the solubility; option C halves it instead of taking the square root.

</details>

---

**Q20.** A fixed mass of gas is held at constant volume. If the absolute temperature is doubled, the pressure:

- A. doubles
- B. halves
- C. quadruples
- D. is unchanged

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** At constant volume, $p \propto T$ in kelvin, so doubling $T$ doubles $p$. Option C squares the factor; option D ignores the direct proportionality. The question is only well posed because the temperature is quoted as absolute.

</details>
