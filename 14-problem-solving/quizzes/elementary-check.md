# Elementary Check

> **Level 1 · 20 questions · 20 minutes**
> **Covers:** chapters 1–9 of [Level 1 — Elementary Chemistry](../../02-elementary-chemistry/README.md).
> **Use:** after finishing Level 1, or as a warm-up before starting Level 2.

Write your answers down before checking any of them. Several questions test a rule *and*

---

**Q1.** The atomic number of an element is the number of:

- A. neutrons in the nucleus
- B. protons in the nucleus
- C. protons plus neutrons
- D. electrons in the outer shell

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The atomic number $Z$ fixes the identity of the element and equals the proton count. Option C is the mass number $A = Z + N$, the most common confusion; option D is the valence count, which varies with ionisation.

</details>

---

**Q2.** A chlorine-37 atom has atomic number 17. How many neutrons does it contain?

- A. 17
- B. 20
- C. 37
- D. 54

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The mass number is the nucleon count, so $N = A - Z = 37 - 17 = 20$. Option A repeats the proton number, and option C repeats the mass number; neither is a neutron count.

</details>

---

**Q3.** Isotopes of an element have:

- A. the same number of neutrons but different numbers of protons
- B. the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons
- C. the same mass number but different atomic numbers
- D. different chemical symbols and different chemistry

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Isotopes share $Z$ and differ in $N$, which is why they occupy the same place in the periodic table and have nearly identical chemistry. Option A reverses the definition — changing the proton count would change the element. Option D is wrong because the chemistry is governed by the electrons, not the neutrons.

</details>

---

**Q4.** What is the electron configuration of a chlorine atom, $Z = 17$?

- A. 2, 8, 8
- B. 2, 8, 7
- C. 2, 7, 8
- D. 2, 8, 6

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Seventeen electrons fill the first shell with 2, the second with 8, leaving 7 in the third. Option A would be the chloride ion, which has gained an electron; option D would be a different element with 16 electrons.

</details>

---

**Q5.** How many orbitals are there in a 2p subshell?

- A. 1
- B. 2
- C. 3
- D. 6

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** A p subshell has $l = 1$, so there are $2l + 1 = 3$ orbitals, conventionally labelled $\mathrm{p_x}$, $\mathrm{p_y}$ and $\mathrm{p_z}$. Option D is the maximum number of *electrons* in the subshell, not the number of orbitals.

</details>

---

**Q6.** What is the maximum number of electrons that the $n = 3$ shell can hold?

- A. 8
- B. 18
- C. 32
- D. 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The capacity is $2n^2 = 2 \times 9 = 18$, made up of 3s (2), 3p (6) and 3d (10). Option A is the capacity of the $n = 2$ shell; option C is the capacity of $n = 4$.

</details>

---

**Q7.** Across period 3 from sodium to chlorine, the atomic radius:

- A. increases
- B. decreases
- C. stays constant
- D. decreases and then increases

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Protons are added to the same shell while the inner shells shield them only imperfectly, so the effective nuclear charge rises and the radius contracts. Option A applies the group trend in the wrong direction; the group trend applies down a column, not across a row.

</details>

---

**Q8.** Which element has the higher first ionisation energy, magnesium or aluminium?

- A. Magnesium
- B. Aluminium
- C. They are equal
- D. It cannot be predicted

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Magnesium loses a $3s$ electron; aluminium loses a $3p$ electron, which is higher in energy and shielded by the filled 3s subshell. Option B follows the general left-to-right increase in nuclear charge and is the attractive distractor, but it ignores the subshell change.

</details>

---

**Q9.** In the formation of sodium chloride from its elements:

- A. sodium gains an electron and chlorine loses one
- B. sodium loses an electron and chlorine gains one
- C. the two atoms share a pair of electrons
- D. both atoms lose electrons

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Sodium has one valence electron beyond a noble-gas core and chlorine is one short of a full shell, so transfer from sodium to chlorine gives both ions a noble-gas configuration. Option C describes covalent bonding, which is not the dominant model for this compound.

</details>

---

**Q10.** What is the formula of magnesium chloride?

- A. $\ce{MgCl}$
- B. $\ce{Mg2Cl}$
- C. $\ce{MgCl2}$
- D. $\ce{Mg2Cl2}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Magnesium forms $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and chlorine forms $\ce{Cl-}$, so two chloride ions are needed for each magnesium ion. Option A would leave a net charge of $+1$; option D has the right ratio but is not the lowest whole-number formula.

</details>

---

**Q11.** A covalent bond is best described as:

- A. a shared pair of electrons between two atoms
- B. an electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
- C. a sea of delocalised electrons surrounding cations
- D. a complete transfer of electrons from one atom to another

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Covalent bonding is the sharing of electron density between nuclei, which lowers the energy of both atoms. Options B and D describe ionic bonding, and option C describes metallic bonding.

</details>

---

**Q12.** Which property is characteristic of a metal but not of an ionic solid?

- A. A high melting point
- B. Conduction of electricity in the solid state
- C. A regular crystalline structure
- D. Solubility in water

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Delocalised electrons in a metal carry current without the lattice having to move, whereas the ions in an ionic solid are fixed and conduct only when molten or dissolved. Option A is shared by many ionic solids, which is why it does not distinguish the two.

</details>

---

**Q13.** In the Lewis structure of ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, the nitrogen atom has:

- A. no lone pairs
- B. one lone pair
- C. two lone pairs
- D. three lone pairs

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Nitrogen has five valence electrons, three of which are shared in N–H bonds, leaving two electrons — one lone pair. Option A is the common error of assuming that every valence electron is used in bonding.

</details>

---

**Q14.** Which of these molecules does not obey the octet rule?

- A. $\ce{H2O}$
- B. $\ce{CH4}$
- C. $\ce{BF3}$
- D. $\ce{NH3}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Boron in $\ce{BF3}$ has only six electrons around it, making it a Lewis acid and an electron-pair acceptor. Option B is complete at eight, and options A and D have full octets with lone pairs on the central atom.

</details>

---

**Q15.** The compound $\ce{Fe2O3}$ is named:

- A. iron(II) oxide
- B. iron(III) oxide
- C. diiron trioxide
- D. iron oxide(II)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Each oxide ion carries $-2$, so three of them give $-6$, which requires two iron ions at $+3$ each. Option A would be $\ce{FeO}$, and option C uses a molecular prefix system that is not used for ionic compounds.

</details>

---

**Q16.** What is the formula of copper(II) sulfate?

- A. $\ce{CuSO4}$
- B. $\ce{Cu2SO4}$
- C. $\ce{CuSO3}$
- D. $\ce{Cu(SO4)2}$

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** $\ce{Cu^2+}$ balances one sulfate ion, $\ce{SO4^2-}$, so the formula is $\ce{CuSO4}$. Option B is copper(I) sulfate, and option C is copper(II) sulfite, a different anion.

</details>

---

**Q17.** Balance: $\ce{CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$. The coefficients are:

- A. 1, 1, 1, 1
- B. 1, 2, 1, 2
- C. 2, 2, 1, 2
- D. 1, 2, 2, 1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** One carbon and four hydrogens on the left require one $\ce{CO2}$ and two $\ce{H2O}$ on the right, which need four oxygen atoms, hence two $\ce{O2}$. Option A leaves two hydrogens and one oxygen unaccounted for.

</details>

---

**Q18.** Balance: $\ce{Al + O2 -> Al2O3}$. The coefficients are:

- A. 2, 3, 1
- B. 4, 3, 2
- C. 2, 1, 1
- D. 4, 2, 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Four aluminium atoms give two $\ce{Al2O3}$ units, which contain six oxygen atoms and therefore require three $\ce{O2}$ molecules. Option A has two aluminium atoms on each side but only three oxygen atoms on the right against six on the left, so it is not balanced.

</details>

---

**Q19.** The pH of $0.010\ \mathrm{mol\ dm^{-3}}$ hydrochloric acid is:

- A. 1
- B. 2
- C. 12
- D. 0.010

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Hydrochloric acid is strong and fully dissociated, so $[\ce{H+}] = 0.010$ and $\mathrm{pH} = -\log(0.010) = 2$. Option D confuses the concentration with the pH, and option C is the pOH.

</details>

---

**Q20.** Chlorine consists of 75 per cent chlorine-35 and 25 per cent chlorine-37. Its relative atomic mass is:

- A. 35.0
- B. 35.5
- C. 36.0
- D. 37.0

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** The weighted mean is $0.75 \times 35 + 0.25 \times 37 = 26.25 + 9.25 = 35.5$. Option C is the unweighted mean of 35 and 37, which would apply only if the two isotopes were equally abundant.

</details>
