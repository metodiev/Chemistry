## Part A — Conceptual questions

### 1. [Ch. 1] Rutherford and the plum-pudding model — 2 marks

**Strategy.** State what the plum-pudding model predicts and what the α-scattering
results actually showed.

**Working.** In the plum-pudding model the positive charge and mass are spread thinly
through the atom, so a fast α particle should pass through with only small deflections.
Most α particles did pass through, but about one in eight thousand was deflected
through more than $90^{\circ}$, which requires a concentrated positive charge and a
large mass.

**Answer.** The plum-pudding model spread the positive charge and mass uniformly through
the atom, so it predicted only slight deflections of α particles. Rutherford observed
that most particles passed through undeflected but a small fraction were scattered
through large angles, even backwards. That requires a tiny, dense, positively charged
nucleus containing almost all the mass, so the plum-pudding model was replaced.

**Check.** The rarity of large deflections is consistent with a nucleus about
$10^{-14}\ \text{m}$ across in an atom about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying the experiment "proved electrons orbit the nucleus";
it located the nucleus, not the electron paths.

### 2. [Ch. 1] Mass number and relative atomic mass — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Distinguish a count of nucleons in one nuclide from a measured,
abundance-weighted mass.

**Working.** The mass number $A$ is the integer sum of protons and neutrons in one
nucleus. The relative atomic mass $A_r$ is the abundance-weighted mean mass of the
element's atoms on the carbon-12 scale, so it is a measured quantity and need not be an
integer.

**Answer.** The mass number $A$ is a count of nucleons in a single nuclide and is always
an integer; for $\ce{^{56}Fe}$ it is 56. The relative atomic mass $A_r$ is a measured
mass on the scale where $\ce{^{12}C}$ is exactly 12, averaged over the natural isotopic
abundance; for iron it is 55.845. The two are approximately equal but not identical,
and only a monoisotopic element has an $A_r$ close to a whole number.

**Check.** For chlorine, $A = 35$ or 37 for the two isotopes, but $A_r = 35.45$ for the
element — the distinction is visible in a single example.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the words "atomic mass" and "mass number"
interchangeably.

### 3. [Ch. 2] Why chlorine is 35.45 — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Explain the weighted mean and give the approximate abundances.

**Working.** Natural chlorine is about $75.76\%$ $\ce{^{35}Cl}$ (relative isotopic mass
34.969) and $24.24\%$ $\ce{^{37}Cl}$ (36.966). The weighted mean,
$0.7576 \times 34.969 + 0.2424 \times 36.966 = 35.45$, lies between the two isotopic
masses.

**Answer.** Chlorine is a mixture of isotopes, so its relative atomic mass is the
abundance-weighted mean of their masses rather than the mass of either one. The mixture
is about three-quarters $\ce{^{35}Cl}$ and one-quarter $\ce{^{37}Cl}$, which places the
mean at 35.45, close to but above 35. A value of exactly 35 would describe a pure
sample of $\ce{^{35}Cl}$, and 37 a pure sample of $\ce{^{37}Cl}$; neither is natural
chlorine.

**Check.** The mean must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotopic masses, and
35.45 does.

**Where marks are lost.** Averaging 35 and 37 without weighting, which gives 36.

### 4. [Ch. 2] Why isotopes behave alike — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Link chemical behaviour to the electron arrangement, not to the nucleus.

**Working.** Isotopes of an element have the same proton count and therefore the same
number of electrons and the same electron configuration. Chemical reactions rearrange
electrons; the neutron count affects mass but not the electron cloud.

**Answer.** Isotopes have identical electron configurations because they have the same
number of protons, and chemistry is controlled by the valence electrons. Their nuclei
differ only in neutron number, which changes the mass and the mass-dependent properties
such as diffusion rate and vibrational frequency. That is why the chemistry is almost
identical, with the small differences concentrated in hydrogen, where the relative mass
change is largest.

**Check.** The kinetic isotope effect is measurable for hydrogen but small for carbon,
oxygen and nitrogen, which matches the size of the relative mass difference.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying the isotopes have "the same number of neutrons".

### 5. [Ch. 3] The three filling rules — 2 marks

**Strategy.** State each rule precisely: what fills first, how electrons occupy
degenerate orbitals, and the exclusion condition.

**Working.** The Aufbau principle: electrons enter the lowest-energy available orbital
first. Hund's rule: within a set of degenerate orbitals, electrons occupy separate
orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. The Pauli exclusion principle: no two
electrons in an atom may share all four quantum numbers, so an orbital holds at most
two electrons with opposite spins.

**Answer.** The Aufbau principle states that orbitals fill in order of increasing
energy. Hund's rule states that degenerate orbitals are singly occupied with parallel
spins before any is doubly occupied. The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two
electrons can have the same set of four quantum numbers, which limits each orbital to
two electrons of opposite spin.

**Check.** The three rules together generate the configuration of nitrogen,
$1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{3}$, with three unpaired $2p$ electrons.

**Where marks are lost.** Stating Hund's rule as "electrons pair up as soon as
possible", which is the opposite of the rule.

### 6. [Ch. 4] Ionisation energy across period 3 — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Identify the two competing factors, then explain the two dips by
subshell structure.

**Working.** Across period 3 the nuclear charge rises while the electrons enter the
same $n = 3$ shell, so the effective nuclear charge rises and the first ionisation
energy generally increases. At aluminium the electron removed is a $3p$ electron, which
lies higher in energy and is better shielded than the $3s$ pair of magnesium. At sulfur
the electron removed is one of a paired $3p$ pair, and the extra electron–electron
repulsion makes it easier to remove than one of the three unpaired $3p$ electrons of
phosphorus.

**Answer.** The general increase from sodium to argon reflects the rising effective
nuclear charge at approximately constant shielding, because the added electrons enter
the same shell. The dip at aluminium occurs because its $3p$ electron is higher in
energy and more shielded than the $3s$ electrons of magnesium. The dip at sulfur occurs
because removing one electron from a $3p$ pair relieves electron–electron repulsion,
whereas phosphorus has three unpaired $3p$ electrons.

**Check.** The first ionisation energies are approximately $496$, $738$, $578$ and
$786\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ for Na, Mg, Al and Si, so the sequence rises except at Al.

**Where marks are lost.** Attributing the aluminium dip to a smaller nuclear charge,
when $Z$ is actually larger than magnesium's.

### 7. [Ch. 5] Three bonding models — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Describe what happens to electrons in each model and link one property to
each.

**Working.** In ionic bonding electrons are transferred, producing cations and anions
held by electrostatic attraction. In covalent bonding electrons are shared in pairs
between atoms. In metallic bonding the valence electrons are delocalised over the whole
lattice of cations.

**Answer.** Ionic bonding involves transfer of electrons and electrostatic attraction
between the resulting ions; it gives high melting points and brittleness. Covalent
bonding involves shared electron pairs, usually between nonmetals; molecular covalent
substances have low melting points and do not conduct when pure. Metallic bonding
involves delocalised electrons shared across a lattice of cations; it gives high
electrical and thermal conductivity and malleability.

**Check.** The three models differ in where the valence electrons are: localised on
ions, shared between two atoms, or delocalised over many atoms.

**Where marks are lost.** Describing metallic bonding as "covalent bonding between
metals".

### 8. [Ch. 6] Why formal charges sum to the net charge — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Show that the bookkeeping neither creates nor destroys electrons.

**Working.** The formal charge counts the valence electrons the free atom would have,
minus the non-bonding electrons it owns and minus one electron for each bond. Summing
over all atoms counts every valence electron once and every bonding pair twice, which
balances the shared-pair convention, so the total equals the net charge of the species.

**Answer.** Formal charge is an accounting device in which each bonding pair is shared
equally, so the sum over all atoms must return the total number of valence electrons
actually present. The total valence electron count is fixed by the atoms and the charge
of the species, so the sum of the formal charges equals that net charge. This is why the
sum is a reliable check on a Lewis structure: a structure whose formal charges do not
sum to the net charge has lost or gained electrons.

**Check.** For $\ce{NO3-}$, the formal charges $+1$, $0$, $-1$ and $-1$ sum to $-1$,
which matches the ion's charge.

**Where marks are lost.** Omitting the charge of a polyatomic ion when counting its
valence electrons.

### 9. [Ch. 7] Why "iron chloride" is incomplete — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Identify the missing information: iron has more than one common cation.

**Working.** Iron forms $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$, so "iron chloride" could mean
$\ce{FeCl2}$ or $\ce{FeCl3}$. The oxidation state must be stated as a Roman numeral
immediately after the metal name.

**Answer.** "Iron chloride" is ambiguous because iron has two common oxidation states,
$+2$ and $+3$, which give different compounds with different formulas and properties.
The systematic name must specify the oxidation state, so $\ce{FeCl2}$ is iron(II)
chloride and $\ce{FeCl3}$ is iron(III) chloride. The Roman numeral is derived from the
formula by balancing the chloride charges.

**Check.** In $\ce{FeCl3}$, three $\ce{Cl-}$ ions carry $-3$ in total, so the single
iron must be $+3$.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing "iron(III)" for $\ce{FeCl2}$ by assuming the higher
state.

### 10. [Ch. 9] Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis acids — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Define each in terms of what is donated or accepted, then give a
proton-free example.

**Working.** A Brønsted–Lowry acid donates a proton; a Lewis acid accepts an electron
pair. Every Brønsted acid is a Lewis acid, because the proton it donates accepts an
electron pair, but the converse is not true.

**Answer.** A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton donor, whereas a Lewis acid is an
electron-pair acceptor. The Lewis definition is broader: boron trifluoride accepts an
electron pair from ammonia to form an adduct, yet it has no proton to donate and is
therefore not a Brønsted acid. Aluminium chloride and carbon dioxide are further
examples of Lewis acids that are not Brønsted acids.

**Check.** The reaction $\ce{BF3 + NH3 -> F3B<-NH3}$ involves no proton transfer, which
is exactly why the Lewis definition is needed.

**Where marks are lost.** Claiming that every Lewis acid is a Brønsted acid.

## Part B — Basic problems

### 11. [Ch. 1] Protons, neutrons and electrons — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Use $N = A - Z$ and $n_{\mathrm{e}} = Z - q$.

**Working.** $\ce{^{23}_{11}Na^+}$: $Z = 11$, $N = 23 - 11 = 12$, and one electron is
lost, so $n_{\mathrm{e}} = 10$. $\ce{^{35}_{17}Cl^-}$: $Z = 17$, $N = 35 - 17 = 18$,
and one electron is gained, so $n_{\mathrm{e}} = 18$. $\ce{^{40}_{20}Ca^2+}$: $Z = 20$,
$N = 40 - 20 = 20$, and two electrons are lost, so $n_{\mathrm{e}} = 18$.

**Answer.** $\ce{^{23}_{11}Na^+}$: 11 p, 12 n, 10 e. $\ce{^{35}_{17}Cl^-}$: 17 p, 18 n,
18 e. $\ce{^{40}_{20}Ca^2+}$: 20 p, 20 n, 18 e.

**Check.** The electron counts reproduce the charges: $11 - 10 = +1$,
$17 - 18 = -1$ and $20 - 18 = +2$.

**Where marks are lost.** Changing the neutron count when the charge changes.

### 12. [Ch. 2] Relative atomic mass of bromine — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Convert the percentages to fractions and take the weighted mean.

**Working.**
$A_r = 0.5069(78.918) + 0.4931(80.916) = 40.004 + 39.900 = 79.903$.

**Answer.** $A_r(\ce{Br}) = 79.90$ (four significant figures).

**Check.** The result lies between 78.918 and 80.916, almost exactly halfway because
the two isotopes are nearly equally abundant.

**Where marks are lost.** Forgetting to divide the percentages by 100, which gives an
answer about 100 times too large.

### 13. [Ch. 3] Configurations of magnesium and sulfur — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Fill orbitals in $(n+\ell)$ order and use the previous noble gas for the
shorthand.

**Working.** Magnesium, $Z = 12$: $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{6}3s^{2}$, shorthand
$[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^{2}$. Sulfur, $Z = 16$:
$1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{6}3s^{2}3p^{4}$, shorthand $[\ce{Ne}]\,3s^{2}3p^{4}$.

**Answer.** (a) Mg: $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{6}3s^{2} = [\ce{Ne}]\,3s^{2}$.
(b) S: $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{6}3s^{2}3p^{4} = [\ce{Ne}]\,3s^{2}3p^{4}$.

**Check.** The superscripts sum to the atomic number: $2+2+6+2 = 12$ and
$2+2+6+2+4 = 16$.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $[\ce{Ar}]$ for sulfur, which already contains the
$3s^{2}3p^{4}$ electrons.

### 14. [Ch. 3] Configurations of manganese cations — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Write the neutral configuration, then remove the $4s$ electrons before
the $3d$ electrons.

**Working.** $\ce{Mn}$ is $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{5}4s^{2}$. Removing the two $4s$ electrons
gives $\ce{Mn^2+}$ as $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{5}$. Removing one further electron from $3d$ gives
$\ce{Mn^3+}$ as $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{4}$.

**Answer.** $\ce{Mn^2+}$: $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{5}$; $\ce{Mn^3+}$: $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{4}$. The
$4s$ electrons are removed first because in the cation the $4s$ orbital is destabilised
relative to $3d$ and the $4s$ electrons are higher in energy.

**Check.** The electron counts are $25 - 2 = 23$ and $25 - 3 = 22$, and $\ce{Mn^2+}$ has
a half-filled $3d^{5}$ subshell.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing $\ce{Mn^2+}$ as $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{3}4s^{2}$ by removing
$3d$ electrons first.

### 15. [Ch. 4] Ranking first ionisation energies down a group — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Apply the down-group trend: the valence electron moves to a higher shell
and is more shielded, so it is easier to remove.

**Working.** Lithium loses a $2s$ electron, sodium a $3s$, potassium a $4s$ and
rubidium a $5s$ electron. The nuclear charge rises down the group, but the added inner
shells shield the valence electron almost completely, and the increase in $n$ lowers the
ionisation energy.

**Answer.** Rb ($403$) $<$ K ($419$) $<$ Na ($496$) $<$ Li ($520$), in
$\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$; the order of increasing first ionisation energy is
$\ce{Rb} < \ce{K} < \ce{Na} < \ce{Li}$.

**Check.** The trend is the opposite of the across-period trend and follows
$E_{\text{i}} \propto Z_{\text{eff}}^{2}/n^{2}$: as $n$ rises, $E_{\text{i}}$ falls.

**Where marks are lost.** Reversing the trend because nuclear charge increases down the
group, while ignoring the increase in $n$ and shielding.

### 16. [Ch. 5] Classifying bonds by electronegativity — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Calculate $\Delta\chi$ and compare with the conventional thresholds.

**Working.** (a) $\ce{K-Cl}$: $3.16 - 0.82 = 2.34$, largely ionic. (b) $\ce{H-F}$:
$3.98 - 2.20 = 1.78$, polar covalent. (c) $\ce{F-F}$: $3.98 - 3.98 = 0$, nonpolar
covalent. (d) $\ce{C-H}$: $2.55 - 2.20 = 0.35$, essentially nonpolar.

**Answer.** (a) Largely ionic; (b) polar covalent; (c) nonpolar covalent;
(d) essentially nonpolar.

**Check.** The thresholds are conventions rather than discontinuities: $\ce{HF}$ has
$\Delta\chi = 1.78$, just above the nominal ionic threshold, yet it is a molecular gas.

**Where marks are lost.** Calling $\ce{HF}$ ionic because its $\Delta\chi$ exceeds 1.7;
the classification predicts bonding character, not the physical state.

### 17. [Ch. 6] Lewis structures of water and carbon dioxide — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Count valence electrons, connect the atoms, complete octets and form
multiple bonds where needed.

**Working.** Water has $2(1) + 6 = 8$ valence electrons: two O–H single bonds and two
lone pairs on oxygen. Carbon dioxide has $4 + 2(6) = 16$ valence electrons: two C=O
double bonds and two lone pairs on each oxygen.

**Answer.** $\ce{H2O}$ has two bonding pairs and two lone pairs on the central oxygen,
with a bond order of 1 and a bent structure, $\ce{H-O-H}$. $\ce{CO2}$ has two double
bonds and no lone pair on the central carbon, with a bond order of 2, two lone pairs on
each oxygen and a linear structure, $\ce{O=C=O}$.

**Check.** In each structure every atom has eight electrons around it (two for each
hydrogen), and the total electron count is conserved.

**Where marks are lost.** Drawing $\ce{CO2}$ with two single bonds, which leaves carbon
with only four valence electrons.

### 18. [Ch. 7] Naming compounds — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Name the cation first, then the anion; use a Roman numeral for a
variable-charge metal and Greek prefixes only for molecular compounds.

**Working.** $\ce{FeCl2}$ contains $\ce{Fe^2+}$, so it is iron(II) chloride.
$\ce{Cr2O3}$ contains $\ce{Cr^3+}$, so it is chromium(III) oxide. $\ce{N2O4}$ is
molecular: dinitrogen tetroxide. $\ce{KClO3}$ contains the chlorate ion, so it is
potassium chlorate.

**Answer.** (a) Iron(II) chloride; (b) chromium(III) oxide; (c) dinitrogen tetroxide;
(d) potassium chlorate.

**Check.** The Roman numerals follow from the anion charges: two chloride ions give
iron $+2$, and three oxide ions give two chromium ions $+3$ each.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing "iron chloride" for (a), or "potassium chlorate(V)"
for (d), which is not the IUPAC name of this ion.

### 19. [Ch. 7] Writing formulas from names — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Identify the ions and balance their charges.

**Working.** Potassium dihydrogenphosphate: $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{H2PO4-}$ give
$\ce{KH2PO4}$. Sodium hydrogensulfate: $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{HSO4-}$ give $\ce{NaHSO4}$.
Calcium sulfate hemihydrate: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ give $\ce{CaSO4}$, with half
a water molecule per formula unit.

**Answer.** (a) $\ce{KH2PO4}$; (b) $\ce{NaHSO4}$; (c) $\ce{CaSO4.0.5H2O}$.

**Check.** Each ionic part is neutral, and the *hemi-* prefix matches the half water
molecule.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing $\ce{CaSO4.0.5H2O}$ as $\ce{Ca(SO4)0.5H2O}$, which
misplaces the water of crystallisation.

### 20. [Ch. 8] Balancing three equations — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Balance carbon and hydrogen first, then oxygen; balance metals and
polyatomic ions before individual nonmetals; clear any fractions.

**Working.**
$$
\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}
$$
Carbon 3, hydrogen 8 and oxygen 10 on both sides.
$$
\ce{Cu + 2AgNO3 -> Cu(NO3)2 + 2Ag}
$$
Copper 1, silver 2, nitrogen 2 and oxygen 6 on both sides.
$$
\ce{3Ca(OH)2 + 2H3PO4 -> Ca3(PO4)2 + 6H2O}
$$
Calcium 3, phosphorus 2, oxygen 14 and hydrogen 12 on both sides.

**Answer.** (a) $1, 5, 3, 4$; (b) $1, 2, 1, 2$; (c) $3, 2, 1, 6$.

**Check.** Each set of coefficients has no common factor, and every element balances
exactly.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $\ce{O}$ instead of $\ce{O2}$ in (a), which makes the
oxygen count impossible to balance.

### 21. [Ch. 9] Conjugate acid–base pairs — 3 marks

**Strategy.** A conjugate pair differs by exactly one proton.

**Working.** $\ce{NH3}$ gains a proton to become $\ce{NH4+}$, so $\ce{NH3}/\ce{NH4+}$
is one pair. $\ce{H2O}$ loses a proton to become $\ce{OH-}$, so $\ce{H2O}/\ce{OH-}$ is
the other. Ammonia accepts the proton and is therefore the base; water donates it and is
the acid.

**Answer.** The pairs are $\ce{NH3}$ (base) and $\ce{NH4+}$ (conjugate acid), and
$\ce{H2O}$ (acid) and $\ce{OH-}$ (conjugate base). Ammonia is the base and water is the
acid in this reaction.

**Check.** The charges balance: neutral $\ce{NH3}$ plus neutral $\ce{H2O}$ gives
$+1$ and $-1$.

**Where marks are lost.** Calling ammonia the acid because it is the substance being
"used as a base" in the laboratory sense, without checking which species donates the
proton.

### 22. [Ch. 8] Three equations for a precipitation — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Write the double displacement, split soluble strong electrolytes, then
cancel the spectator ions.

**Working.** The molecular equation is
$$
\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)}
$$
The complete ionic equation splits the soluble salts:
$$
\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq) + 2K+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2K+(aq) + 2NO3-(aq)}
$$
Cancelling $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ leaves the net ionic equation.

**Answer.** Molecular:
$$
\ce{Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2KI(aq) -> PbI2(s) + 2KNO3(aq)}
$$
Net ionic:
$$
\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> PbI2(s)}
$$

**Check.** Atoms and charges balance: one Pb and two I on each side, with a total charge
of zero on both sides.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing $\ce{PbI2}$ as aqueous, which removes the driving
force for the reaction.

## Part C — Intermediate problems

### 23. [Ch. 1] Volume ratio of atom to nucleus — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Volume scales as the cube of the radius.

**Working.**
$$
\frac{V_{\text{atom}}}{V_{\text{nucleus}}}
= \left(\frac{r_{\text{atom}}}{r_{\text{nucleus}}}\right)^{3}
= \left(\frac{1.0 \times 10^{-10}}{1.0 \times 10^{-14}}\right)^{3}
= (10^{4})^{3} = 10^{12}
$$

**Answer.** The atomic volume is about $10^{12}$ times the nuclear volume, so the
nucleus occupies about one part in a trillion of the atom's volume. The atom is
therefore mostly empty space.

**Check.** The ratio of radii is $10^{4}$, and cubing it gives $10^{12}$, consistent
with the order-of-magnitude estimate quoted in the chapter.

**Where marks are lost.** Reporting $10^{4}$ by forgetting to cube the radius ratio.

### 24. [Ch. 2] Abundances of boron isotopes — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Let $x$ be the fraction of $\ce{^{10}B}$ and $1 - x$ the fraction of
$\ce{^{11}B}$, then solve the weighted-mean equation.

**Working.**
$$
10.81 = 10.013x + 11.009(1 - x)
$$
$$
10.81 = 11.009 - 0.996x
$$
$$
x = \frac{11.009 - 10.81}{0.996} = 0.1998
$$

**Answer.** $x = 0.1998$, so the abundances are $20.0\%$ $\ce{^{10}B}$ and $80.0\%$
$\ce{^{11}B}$.

**Check.** $0.200(10.013) + 0.800(11.009) = 2.003 + 8.807 = 10.81$, which reproduces
the given relative atomic mass.

**Where marks are lost.** Reporting $20\%$ as the abundance of $\ce{^{11}B}$ rather than
$\ce{^{10}B}$, which reverses the two percentages.

### 25. [Ch. 3] Copper, zinc and the filled $3d$ subshell — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Write the predicted configurations, then compare the observed ones and
identify the stabilisation that operates in copper but not in zinc.

**Working.** The $(n+\ell)$ rule predicts $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{9}4s^{2}$ for copper and
$[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^{2}$ for zinc. The observed configurations are
$[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^{1}$ for copper and the predicted $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^{2}$ for
zinc. Copper gains a filled $3d^{10}$ subshell by promoting one $4s$ electron, and the
$3d$–$4s$ gap is small enough for this to be favourable. Zinc already has a filled
$3d^{10}$ subshell, so there is no $3d$ vacancy into which a $4s$ electron could be
promoted; emptying the $4s$ subshell would raise the energy, and no anomaly is observed.

**Answer.** $\ce{Cu}$: $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^{1}$; $\ce{Zn}$: $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^{2}$;
$\ce{Cu+}$: $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{10}$. Copper is anomalous because the filled $3d^{10}$
configuration is stabilised; zinc is not because it already has that configuration
together with a filled $4s$ subshell.

**Check.** The superscripts sum to the atomic number: $18+10+1 = 29$ for copper and
$18+10+2 = 30$ for zinc.

**Where marks are lost.** Claiming that "half-filled and filled subshells are always
more stable" without checking whether the alternative actually leaves a filled or
half-filled arrangement.

### 26. [Ch. 3] Using the $(n+\ell)$ rule — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Compute $n+\ell$ for each subshell and break ties by the lower $n$.

**Working.** $4s$: $4+0 = 4$. $3d$: $3+2 = 5$. $4p$: $4+1 = 5$. $5s$: $5+0 = 5$.
$4d$: $4+2 = 6$. The order is $4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d$.

**Answer.** $4s$, $3d$, $4p$, $5s$, $4d$. The configuration
$[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{5}4s^{1}$ has $18 + 6 = 24$ electrons, so the element is chromium.

**Check.** The same order appears in the chapter's full filling sequence, and chromium's
configuration is the known anomaly at $Z = 24$.

**Where marks are lost.** Ordering $4p$ before $3d$ because both have $n+\ell = 5$;
the tie is broken by the lower $n$.

### 27. [Ch. 4] Isoelectronic series — 4 marks

**Strategy.** For a fixed electron count, radius falls as nuclear charge rises.

**Working.** All four species have the argon configuration, with 18 electrons. Their
nuclear charges are 16, 17, 19 and 20 for $\ce{S^2-}$, $\ce{Cl^-}$, $\ce{K+}$ and
$\ce{Ca^2+}$. The greater the nuclear charge, the more strongly the same 18 electrons
are attracted.

**Answer.** $\ce{S^2-} > \ce{Cl^-} > \ce{K+} > \ce{Ca^2+}$.

**Check.** The anion with the smallest nuclear charge is the largest, and the cation
with the largest nuclear charge is the smallest, as the trend requires.

**Where marks are lost.** Ranking by the periodic table position without noticing that
the species are isoelectronic.

### 28. [Ch. 5] Why MgO is more refractory than KBr — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Use the proportionality of lattice enthalpy to charge product and inverse
distance.

**Working.** For MgO, $z_+z_- = (+2)(-2) = 4$ and
$r_+ + r_- = 72 + 140 = 212\ \text{pm}$. For KBr, $z_+z_- = (+1)(-1) = 1$ and
$r_+ + r_- = 138 + 196 = 334\ \text{pm}$.
The ratio is
$\frac{4/212}{1/334} = \frac{0.01887}{0.002994} = 6.30$.

**Answer.** The lattice enthalpy of MgO is about $6.3$ times that of KBr, so much more
energy is needed to separate the ions and the melting point is far higher. The dominant
factor is the product of the charges, which is four times larger; the smaller
internuclear distance adds a further factor of about $1.6$.

**Check.** The estimate is consistent with the measured lattice enthalpies
($3795$ against $682\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, a ratio of $5.6$) and with melting points of
about $2852\ ^{\circ}$ C for MgO and $734\ ^{\circ}$ C for KBr.

**Where marks are lost.** Comparing only the charges and ignoring the distance term, or
doubling one charge instead of multiplying the two.

### 30. [Ch. 6] Formal charges in the nitrate ion — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Count valence electrons, then apply $FC = V - N_{\text{lone}} - N_{\text{bonds}}$
to each atom.

**Working.** $\ce{NO3-}$ has $5 + 3(6) + 1 = 24$ valence electrons. With one double bond
and two single bonds, nitrogen has four bonds and no lone pair, so
$FC(\ce{N}) = 5 - 0 - 4 = +1$. The double-bonded oxygen has two lone pairs and two
bonds: $6 - 4 - 2 = 0$. Each single-bonded oxygen has three lone pairs and one bond:
$6 - 6 - 1 = -1$.

**Answer.** $FC(\ce{N}) = +1$, $FC(\ce{O}_{\text{double}}) = 0$ and
$FC(\ce{O}_{\text{single}}) = -1$ for each of the two single-bonded oxygens; the sum is
$+1 + 0 - 1 - 1 = -1$, the charge of the ion. The double bond can be placed on any of
the three oxygens, giving three equivalent resonance structures; the real ion is a
hybrid with three equal N–O bonds of order $4/3 \approx 1.33$.

**Check.** The formal charges sum to the net charge, and the resonance hybrid has higher
symmetry than any single structure, which matches the observed equal bond lengths.

**Where marks are lost.** Forgetting the extra electron of the anion, which gives 23
valence electrons and an impossible structure.

### 35. [Ch. 1] Nuclear radius and density of aluminium-27 — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Use $R = r_0A^{1/3}$, then the volume of a sphere and
$\rho = m/V$ with the nuclear mass approximated as $A m_u$.

**Working.** $A^{1/3} = 27^{1/3} = 3.000$, so
$$
R = r_0A^{1/3} = (1.2 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m})(3.000)
= 3.60 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m}
$$
$$
V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3} = 4.189(3.60 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m})^{3}
= 1.95 \times 10^{-43}\ \text{m}^{3}
$$
$$
m = Am_u = 27(1.6605 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}) = 4.48 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}
$$
$$
\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{4.48 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}}{1.95 \times 10^{-43}\ \text{m}^{3}}
= 2.29 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}
$$

**Answer.** $R = 3.60 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$,
$V = 1.95 \times 10^{-43}\ \text{m}^{3}$,
$m = 4.48 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$ and
$\rho = 2.29 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$.

**Check.** The density is about $2.3 \times 10^{14}$ times that of water and agrees
with the value obtained for iron-56 in the chapter's worked example. Because $A$
cancels in the density expression, all nuclei should have similar densities, which is
the physical content of the $A^{1/3}$ law.

**Where marks are lost.** Using the atomic diameter instead of the radius, or
forgetting to cube the radius when computing the volume.

### 37. [Ch. 3] The Hα line — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Apply the Rydberg equation for $n_1 = 2$, $n_2 = 3$, then convert the
wavelength to energy with $E = hc/\lambda$.

**Working.**
$$
\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{2^{2}} - \frac{1}{3^{2}}\right)
= (1.097 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1})\left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{9}\right)
= 1.524 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^{-1}
$$
$$
\lambda = \frac{1}{1.524 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}^{-1}}
= 6.56 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 656\ \text{nm}
$$
$$
E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
= \frac{(6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1})}
{6.56 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}}
= 3.03 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}
$$

**Answer.** $\lambda = 6.56 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$ (656 nm) and
$E = 3.03 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$ per photon. The wavelength lies in the red part of
the visible spectrum.

**Check.** The Balmer series lies in the visible, and 656 nm is the familiar red line of
hydrogen; the photon energy is about $1.9\ \text{eV}$, well below the $13.6\ \text{eV}$
ionisation energy of hydrogen.

**Where marks are lost.** Using $n_1 = 1$ and $n_2 = 3$, which gives the Lyman series in
the ultraviolet, not the visible Hα line.

### 38. [Ch. 4] Successive ionisation energies of magnesium — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Calculate the ratios, then interpret each large jump as the start of a new
inner shell.

**Working.**
$\text{IE}_2/\text{IE}_1 = 1451/738 = 1.97$;
$\text{IE}_3/\text{IE}_2 = 7733/1451 = 5.33$;
$\text{IE}_{11}/\text{IE}_{10} = 169\,988/35\,469 = 4.79$.
The first large jump comes after two electrons have been removed, so there are two

### 39. [Ch. 5] Lattice enthalpy of potassium chloride — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Build the Born–Haber cycle:
$\Delta H_{\text{f}} = \Delta H_{\text{sub}} + \text{IE}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2}D + \Delta H_{\text{eg}} + \Delta H_{\text{latt}}$.

**Working.** The cycle runs from the elements in their standard states to the gaseous
ions: sublimation of potassium, ionisation of potassium, dissociation of chlorine,
electron gain by chlorine, and finally lattice formation. Substituting the data:
$$
-436.5 = 89 + 419 + 122 + (-349) + \Delta H_{\text{latt}}
$$
The sum of the four positive and negative terms is $281$, so
$$
\Delta H_{\text{latt}} = -436.5 - 281 = -717.5\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}
$$

**Answer.** $\Delta H_{\text{latt}} = -718\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ (three significant
figures). The lattice term is negative because the gaseous ions are attracted to one
another and assemble into the solid, releasing energy; the formation of the lattice is
the dominant exothermic step of the cycle.

**Check.** The lattice enthalpy of KCl is about $-715\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ in standard
tables, so the cycle agrees to better than $1\%$; the formation enthalpy of
$-436.5\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ is reproduced.

**Where marks are lost.** Adding the electron affinity with the wrong sign, which gives
$-1416\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$, about twice the accepted magnitude.

### 40. [Ch. 5, Ch. 6] Two Lewis structures for sulfur dioxide — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Count valence electrons, draw both skeletons, then evaluate
$FC = V - N_{\text{lone}} - N_{\text{bonds}}$.

**Working.** $\ce{SO2}$ has $6 + 2(6) = 18$ valence electrons. With one double bond,
sulfur has one lone pair and three bonds, so
$FC(\ce{S}) = 6 - 2 - 3 = +1$; the single-bonded oxygen has three lone pairs and one
bond, $FC = 6 - 6 - 1 = -1$. With two double bonds, sulfur has one lone pair and four
bonds, so $FC(\ce{S}) = 6 - 2 - 4 = 0$, and each oxygen has
$6 - 4 - 2 = 0$.

**Answer.** The one-double-bond structure gives sulfur $+1$ and one oxygen $-1$; the
two-double-bond structure gives every atom a formal charge of zero. The second
structure is preferred because it minimises formal charge, even though sulfur then has
ten electrons in its valence shell. The expanded octet is best treated as a
bookkeeping device: the S–O bonds are polar and partly ionic, and the additional
electron density is not a literal promotion into $3d$ orbitals.

**Check.** Both structures have the same total valence electron count and a net charge
of zero, but only the second avoids charge separation.

**Where marks are lost.** Claiming that sulfur "must" have eight electrons, which
forces the charged structure.

### 41. [Ch. 9] Strong and weak acids — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Use the pH difference to find the ratio of hydronium concentrations, as in

**Working.**
$$
\Delta\text{pH} = 2.87 - 1.00 = 1.87
$$
$$
\frac{[\ce{H3O+}]_{\ce{HCl}}}{[\ce{H3O+}]_{\text{ethanoic}}}
= 10^{1.87} = 7.4 \times 10^{1}

[\ce{H3O+}]_{\text{ethanoic}} = \frac{0.100}{74}

$$
$$
\text{percentage ionised} = \frac{1.35 \times 10^{-3}}{0.100} \times 100\%

$$

**Check.** A weak acid at $0.100\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ with about $1\%$ ionisation gives

the concentration ratio as $2.87 - 1.00 = 1.87$.

### 42. [Ch. 8] Limiting reagent in magnesium combustion — 7 marks

**Strategy.** Convert both masses to amounts, divide by the stoichiometric coefficients,
and compare.

**Working.**
$$
n(\ce{Mg}) = \frac{12.0\ \text{g}}{24.305\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 0.4937\ \text{mol}
$$
$$
n(\ce{O2}) = \frac{8.00\ \text{g}}{31.998\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 0.2500\ \text{mol}
$$
From $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$, oxygen requires $2 \times 0.2500 = 0.5000\ \text{mol}$ of
Mg, but only $0.4937\ \text{mol}$ is available, so magnesium is limiting.

Then
$$
n(\ce{MgO}) = n(\ce{Mg}) = 0.4937\ \text{mol},
\qquad m(\ce{MgO}) = (0.4937)(40.304) = 19.9\ \text{g}
$$
Oxygen consumed is $0.4937/2 = 0.2469\ \text{mol}$, so the excess is
$0.2500 - 0.2469 = 0.0031\ \text{mol}$, or $0.101\ \text{g}$.

**Answer.** Magnesium is the limiting reagent, $19.9\ \text{g}$ of magnesium oxide is
formed, and $0.101\ \text{g}$ of oxygen remains.

**Check.** Conservation of mass: $12.0\ \text{g}$ Mg plus $(8.00 - 0.101) = 7.90\ \text{g}$
of oxygen that reacted gives $19.9\ \text{g}$ of product, which matches the calculated
mass of MgO.

**Where marks are lost.** Comparing the masses directly ($12.0\ \text{g}$ against
$8.00\ \text{g}$) instead of comparing amounts, which wrongly identifies oxygen as
limiting.

## Part F — Multiple-choice answers

### 51. [Ch. 1] Evidence for the nucleus — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Decide what each model predicts for α particles and match it against the
observed distribution of scattering angles.

**Working.** A diffuse positive charge would deflect α particles only slightly, so large
deflections would be rare or absent. The observed backward scattering requires a
concentrated charge and a large mass in a small volume. Deflection of cathode rays shows
that electrons are charged, line spectra show quantised energy levels, and electron
diffraction shows wave behaviour.

**Answer.** B. Most α particles pass through undeflected, but a few are deflected
through large angles.

**Check.** The proportion of large-angle deflections is about one in eight thousand,
consistent with a nucleus some $10^{-4}$ of the atomic diameter.

**Where marks are lost.** Choosing C or D because they are famous experiments, without
noting that neither probes the nuclear mass and charge distribution.

### 52. [Ch. 3] Configuration of chromium — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Recall that chromium is one of the two first-row anomalies and that its
half-filled $3d$ subshell is lower in energy than the predicted arrangement.

**Working.** The simple filling order gives $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{4}4s^{2}$, but the observed
ground state is $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{5}4s^{1}$. The half-filled $3d^{5}$ configuration gains
exchange stabilisation, and the $3d$–$4s$ energy gap is small.

**Answer.** B. $[\ce{Ar}]\,3d^{5}4s^{1}$.

**Check.** The superscripts sum to $18 + 5 + 1 = 24$, the atomic number of chromium.

**Where marks are lost.** Choosing A, the configuration predicted by the simple filling
order, which is the most common error in this topic.

### 53. [Ch. 4] Largest atomic radius in period 3 — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Across a period, the shell number is constant while the nuclear charge
rises, so radius falls from left to right.

**Working.** Sodium, magnesium, aluminium and silicon all have their valence electrons
in $n = 3$, but the nuclear charge rises from 11 to 14. The increasing effective nuclear
charge pulls the same shell inward, so sodium is largest and silicon smallest.

**Answer.** A. Sodium.

**Check.** The order of decreasing radius, Na $>$ Mg $>$ Al $>$ Si, is the reverse of
the order of increasing effective nuclear charge.

**Where marks are lost.** Choosing D because silicon is the metalloid and "metalloids
are large", which reverses the trend.

### 54. [Ch. 7] Name of $\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}$ — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Derive the oxidation state of iron from the anion charges, then apply the
Roman-numeral convention for variable-charge metals.

**Working.** Three sulfate ions carry $3(-2) = -6$ in total, so the two iron ions must
carry $+6$ between them and each is $\ce{Fe^3+}$. The name is iron(III) sulfate;
"iron(II) sulfate" is $\ce{FeSO4}$, and prefixes such as "diiron" belong to molecular
compounds.

**Answer.** C. Iron(III) sulfate.

**Check.** In $\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}$, $2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0$, so the formula and the name agree.

**Where marks are lost.** Choosing A by omitting the oxidation state, or B by halving
the iron charge.

### 55. [Ch. 9] pH difference — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Convert the pH difference into a power of ten, because each pH unit is a
tenfold change in hydronium concentration.

**Working.** The difference is $5 - 3 = 2$ units, so the concentration ratio is
$10^{2} = 100$. A ratio of 2 would be correct only if pH were a linear scale.

**Answer.** C. $100$ times.

**Check.** pH 3 corresponds to $1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$ and pH 5 to
$1 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{mol dm}^{-3}$; the ratio is indeed $100$.

**Where marks are lost.** Choosing A by subtracting the pH values and treating the
difference as a ratio.

## Top ten errors in this level

| # | Error | Why it happens | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Writing $4s$ after $3d$ when writing the configuration of a transition-metal ion | The filling order is memorised as a sequence and applied to ions | Remove $4s$ electrons first; write $3d$ before $4s$ in the final configuration |
| 2 | Using the wrong noble-gas core | The shorthand is chosen by habit rather than by the previous noble gas | Count back to the nearest lower atomic number |
| 3 | Treating $\Delta\chi > 1.7$ as proof of an ionic solid | The threshold is a convention, not a discontinuity | Check the physical state and lattice energy; HF is molecular |
| 4 | Omitting lone pairs from a Lewis structure | Bonds are drawn first and the remaining electrons are forgotten | Count the total valence electrons and account for every one |
| 5 | Forgetting the charge when counting valence electrons of an ion | The formula is read as neutral | Add one electron per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge |
| 6 | Putting Greek prefixes into an ionic name | Molecular and ionic naming rules are mixed | Prefixes belong to molecular compounds only |
| 7 | Omitting the Roman numeral for a variable-charge metal | The formula is read without deriving the oxidation state | Derive the state from the anion charges |
| 8 | Using $\ce{O}$ or $\ce{Cl}$ instead of $\ce{O2}$ or $\ce{Cl2}$ | The element symbol is written instead of the molecular formula | Check the standard state before balancing |
| 9 | Leaving a fractional coefficient in a final equation | The fraction is accepted as a coefficient | Multiply through by the denominator |
| 10 | Treating pH as a linear scale | The logarithm is forgotten under examination pressure | Convert pH differences to powers of ten before comparing concentrations |
