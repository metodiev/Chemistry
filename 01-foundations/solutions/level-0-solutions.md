## Part A — Conceptual questions

### 1. [Ch. 1] Chemistry, chemical change and physical change — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Define chemistry by its four objects of study, then separate the two
kinds of change by the criterion of whether new substances are formed.

**Working.** Chemistry studies the composition, structure and properties of matter and
the transformations it undergoes. A chemical change produces one or more new
substances whose composition and properties differ from those of the starting
materials. A physical change alters form, state or appearance while the chemical
identity is retained.

**Answer.** Chemistry is the study of matter — its composition, structure, properties
and transformations. Burning methane is a chemical change: the products, carbon dioxide
and water, are new substances with different compositions, and the change cannot be
undone by a physical method. Ice melting is a physical change: the substance remains
$\ce{H2O}$, and refreezing recovers the original solid. The evidence for the
classification is therefore a change in chemical identity, not a change in appearance.

**Check.** A colour change alone would not be decisive, because dissolving a coloured
salt also changes the appearance of the water without producing a new substance.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing "a chemical change cannot be reversed" as the
definition; many chemical changes are reversible, and many physical changes are not.

### 2. [Ch. 2] The operational test for purity — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Use the separation test: a mixture can be split by a physical process, a
pure substance cannot.

**Working.** Evaporation, filtration, distillation and chromatography separate
components without breaking chemical bonds. If such a method yields two or more
substances with different properties, the original sample was a mixture. If no
physical method separates the sample, it is a pure substance; separating it requires a
chemical reaction.

**Answer.** Salt water is a mixture: evaporation or distillation separates it into
water and sodium chloride, and the salt can be recovered unchanged. Pure water is a
compound: no physical process separates it into simpler substances, and the only way to
decompose it is electrolysis, a chemical change that gives hydrogen and oxygen. The
operational test is therefore physical separability, not visual uniformity.

**Check.** Salt water also boils over a range of temperature and above $100\ ^\circ$
C at $1\ \text{atm}$, whereas pure water boils sharply at $100\ ^\circ$ C.

**Where marks are lost.** Classifying a clear, uniform solution as pure because
"nothing can be seen in it".

### 3. [Ch. 3] Why solids keep their shape and gases do not — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Compare thermal energy with the strength of intermolecular attraction in
each state.

**Working.** In a solid the thermal energy of the particles is small compared with the
attractive forces between them, so the particles vibrate about fixed average positions
and the lattice resists deformation. In a gas the thermal energy greatly exceeds the
attraction, so the particles travel freely, collide elastically and occupy whatever
volume is available.

**Answer.** A solid has a definite shape because its particles are held in fixed
average positions by intermolecular attraction that is stronger than their thermal
energy; they can vibrate but not migrate. A gas fills its container because its
particles have thermal energy far larger than any attraction between them, so they move
independently until they hit the walls. The same balance explains the liquid case, in
which the two quantities are comparable and the particles stay in contact while sliding
past one another.

**Check.** Heating a solid eventually supplies enough thermal energy to overcome the
attraction, and it melts — the model predicts the observed transition.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying "gas particles are not attracted to each other at
all"; the model treats attraction as negligible compared with thermal energy, not
absent.

### 4. [Ch. 4] Intensive and extensive properties — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Test whether the value changes when the sample is divided.

**Working.** An extensive property is proportional to the amount of material; an
intensive property is independent of amount. Mass doubles when the sample doubles, so
it is extensive. Density is the ratio of two extensive quantities, $m/V$, so it is
intensive.

**Answer.** An intensive property does not depend on the amount of substance, whereas
an extensive property does. Mass is extensive: two identical pieces have twice the
mass of one. Density is intensive: cutting a block in half leaves the ratio $m/V$
unchanged. Density can therefore identify a substance, because its value is a
fingerprint of the material rather than of the sample; mass alone says only how much
material is present.

**Check.** Specific heat capacity and concentration are intensive for the same reason:
each is a ratio of two extensive quantities.

**Where marks are lost.** Classifying volume as intensive because "it is a property of
the object".

### 5. [Ch. 5] Element, compound and mixture — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Distinguish by the number of atom types, by whether the components are
chemically bonded in a fixed ratio, and by whether physical separation is possible.

**Working.** An element contains one kind of atom. A compound contains two or more
elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio by mass. A mixture contains two or more
substances that are not chemically combined, in a composition that can vary.

**Answer.** Iron is an element: it consists of one kind of atom and cannot be
decomposed chemically. Water is a compound: hydrogen and oxygen are chemically bonded
in a fixed $2:1$ atom ratio, and its properties are not the average of those of the
elements. Salt water is a mixture: the sodium chloride and water are not bonded to each
other in a fixed ratio, the composition can be varied, and evaporation separates the
components. The structural distinction is that a compound has chemical bonds between
its constituent elements, whereas a mixture does not.

**Check.** A mixture can be separated by a physical method; a compound cannot, which is
the operational version of the same distinction.

**Where marks are lost.** Calling air a compound because it is "made of gases".

### 6. [Ch. 6] Neutral atoms and ions — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Use $q = (Z - n_{\mathrm{e}})e$: the charge is the proton count minus the
electron count.

**Working.** A neutral chlorine atom has $Z = 17$ protons and 17 electrons, so
$17 - 17 = 0$. The chloride ion has gained one electron, so it has 17 protons and 18
electrons and a net charge of $-1$.

**Answer.** An atom is neutral because its number of positively charged protons equals
its number of negatively charged electrons, so the charges cancel exactly. An ion forms
when electrons are gained or lost while the nucleus is unchanged, so the two counts no
longer match. A chlorine atom has 17 protons and 17 electrons; a chloride ion has 17
protons and 18 electrons, giving a net charge of $-1$.

**Check.** The nucleus is untouched by ionisation, which is why the element remains
chlorine in both species.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying the ion "has one extra proton" — the proton count of
an element never changes in a chemical process.

### 7. [Ch. 7] Why the table is ordered by atomic number — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Show that mass ordering produces a chemical inversion and that atomic
number removes it.

**Working.** Argon has $A_r = 39.95$ and potassium $A_r = 39.10$, so ordering by
increasing mass would place potassium (19) before argon (18). But argon is a noble gas
with a full valence shell and potassium is a reactive alkali metal, so the chemistry
demands the opposite order. Ordering by $Z$ places argon at 18 and potassium at 19 and
reproduces the chemistry.

**Answer.** Mendeleev ordered the elements by relative atomic mass, which worked for
most pairs but inverted argon and potassium: mass puts potassium first, while their
chemistry puts argon first. Moseley showed in 1913 that the frequency of an element's
characteristic X-rays increases smoothly with an integer, the atomic number, and
ordering by that integer removes the inversion. The periodic law is therefore stated in
terms of increasing atomic number, because $Z$ is the proton count that determines the
electron arrangement and hence the chemistry.

**Check.** The same argument resolves the cobalt–nickel and tellurium–iodine
inversions.

**Where marks are lost.** Claiming that Mendeleev "knew about protons"; atomic number
was unknown in 1869.

### 8. [Ch. 8] Subscript and coefficient — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Compare what each number multiplies.

**Working.** A subscript belongs only to the symbol immediately before it and is part
of the formula. A coefficient multiplies the whole formula and states how many formula
units are present.

**Answer.** In $\ce{H2O2}$ the subscripts are part of the formula: one molecule of
hydrogen peroxide contains two hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. In $\ce{2H2O}$ the
coefficient 2 multiplies the whole formula, so there are two water molecules, four
hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms in total. Changing a coefficient changes the amount
of a substance; changing a subscript changes the substance itself, because
$\ce{H2O2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ are different compounds with different properties.

**Check.** The hydrogen peroxide molecule has four atoms in total, whereas the two
water molecules have six, so the two expressions are not interchangeable.

**Where marks are lost.** Reading $\ce{2H2O}$ as "two hydrogen atoms and two oxygen
atoms".

### 9. [Ch. 9] Accuracy, precision and systematic error — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Define each term by its reference — the true value for accuracy, other
measurements for precision.

**Working.** Accuracy is closeness to the true or accepted value; precision is
closeness of repeated measurements to one another. A systematic error shifts every
reading in the same direction by a roughly constant amount, so the mean is displaced by
that amount.

**Answer.** Accuracy measures how close a result is to the true value, while precision
measures how closely repeated measurements agree with each other. A systematic error,
such as a balance reading $0.05\ \text{g}$ too high, affects accuracy because it shifts
all readings in the same direction. Averaging cannot remove it: the mean of a constant
offset is the same offset, so the result remains displaced even though the scatter is
reduced.

**Check.** Random errors scatter in both directions, so they affect precision and are
reduced by averaging — the opposite behaviour.

**Where marks are lost.** Using "accurate" and "precise" as synonyms.

### 10. [Ch. 10] Hazard, risk and the hierarchy of control — 2 marks

**Strategy.** Separate the intrinsic property (hazard) from the situation-dependent
probability (risk), then recall the order of controls.

**Working.** A hazard is a source of potential harm; a risk is the probability and
severity of harm occurring in a particular situation. The hierarchy runs from
elimination and substitution, through engineering controls, to administrative controls
and finally personal protective equipment.

**Answer.** A hazard is the intrinsic potential of a substance or activity to cause
harm, whereas a risk is the chance and severity of that harm in a specific situation;
concentrated acid is hazardous, but the risk is low if it is used in a fume hood with
small quantities. The first level of control is elimination — removing the hazard, for
example by demonstrating with a simulation instead of the reagent. The second is
substitution — replacing the hazard with something safer, for example using dilute acid
where the concentrated acid is not required.

**Check.** Personal protective equipment is the last level, not the first, because it
protects only the wearer and depends on correct use.

**Where marks are lost.** Listing "wear goggles" as the first control.

## Part B — Basic problems

### 11. [Ch. 2] Classifying samples — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Ask two questions in order: is there one substance or more? If more, is
the composition uniform to the particle scale?

**Working.** Air is a uniform mixture of gases. Carbon dioxide is a compound of two
elements. Brass is a uniform solid mixture of copper and zinc. Diamond is a structural
form of the element carbon. Milk is a colloid, uniform to the eye but heterogeneous at
the particle scale. Granite contains visible crystals of different minerals.

**Answer.** (a) Air — homogeneous mixture; (b) carbon dioxide — compound;
(c) brass — homogeneous mixture; (d) diamond — element; (e) milk — heterogeneous
mixture (colloid); (f) granite — heterogeneous mixture.

**Check.** Only carbon dioxide has a fixed composition by mass; the other samples can
vary in composition, which is the signature of a mixture.

**Where marks are lost.** Calling brass a compound because it is a solid alloy, or
diamond a compound because it is a network solid.

### 12. [Ch. 4] Physical or chemical change — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Look for a new substance, not for a change of appearance.

**Working.** Melting, dissolving and evaporating recover the original substance by a
physical process. Rusting, burning and heating limestone produce new substances with
different compositions.

**Answer.** (a) Physical; (b) chemical; (c) physical; (d) chemical; (e) physical;
(f) chemical.

**Check.** Iron rusting gives a hydrated iron oxide, methane burning gives carbon
dioxide and water, and heated limestone gives calcium oxide and carbon dioxide — all
new substances.

**Where marks are lost.** Marking dissolving as chemical because the solid disappears.

### 13. [Ch. 3] Naming phase changes — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Name the transition by its initial and final states; endothermic
transitions increase the freedom of the particles.

**Working.** Solid to gas is sublimation; gas to liquid is condensation; liquid to
solid is freezing; solid to liquid is melting.

**Answer.** (a) Sublimation, endothermic; (b) condensation, exothermic; (c) freezing,
exothermic; (d) melting, endothermic.

**Check.** Every transition that separates or frees particles requires energy, and
every reverse transition releases it.

**Where marks are lost.** Confusing sublimation with evaporation, or calling melting
exothermic because "heat is applied".

### 14. [Ch. 4] Intensive or extensive — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Double the sample mentally and see whether the value doubles.

**Working.** Mass, volume and heat capacity double with the sample. Density, melting
point and colour do not.

**Answer.** (a) Extensive; (b) intensive; (c) intensive; (d) extensive; (e) intensive;
(f) extensive.

**Check.** Density is $m/V$, a ratio of two extensive quantities, and specific heat
capacity is heat capacity per unit mass, so both are intensive.

**Where marks are lost.** Classifying heat capacity as intensive and specific heat
capacity as extensive — the reverse is true.

### 15. [Ch. 6] Protons, neutrons and electrons — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Use $N = A - Z$ and $n_{\mathrm{e}} = Z - q$.

**Working.** For $\ce{^{32}_{16}S^2-}$: $Z = 16$, $A = 32$, so $N = 16$; the charge is
$-2$, so $n_{\mathrm{e}} = 16 - (-2) = 18$. For $\ce{^{27}_{13}Al^3+}$: $Z = 13$,
$A = 27$, so $N = 14$; the charge is $+3$, so $n_{\mathrm{e}} = 13 - 3 = 10$.

**Answer.** $\ce{^{32}_{16}S^2-}$ has 16 protons, 16 neutrons and 18 electrons.
$\ce{^{27}_{13}Al^3+}$ has 13 protons, 14 neutrons and 10 electrons.

**Check.** Both ions have a noble-gas electron count: 18 for sulfide (argon) and 10 for
aluminium(III) (neon).

**Where marks are lost.** Changing the neutron count when the charge changes; the
nucleus is unaffected by ionisation.

### 16. [Ch. 6] Molecular and empirical formulas — 3 marks

**Strategy.** The molecular formula counts atoms in one molecule; the empirical
formula divides by the highest common factor.

**Working.** Hydrogen peroxide is $\ce{H2O2}$, which reduces to $\ce{HO}$. Glucose is
$\ce{C6H12O6}$, which reduces to $\ce{CH2O}$. Ethene is $\ce{C2H4}$, which reduces to
$\ce{CH2}$.

**Answer.** (a) $\ce{H2O2}$, empirical $\ce{HO}$, 4 atoms per molecule;
(b) $\ce{C6H12O6}$, empirical $\ce{CH2O}$, 24 atoms per molecule; (c) $\ce{C2H4}$,
empirical $\ce{CH2}$, 6 atoms per molecule.

**Check.** Each molecular formula is a whole-number multiple of its empirical formula:
$\ce{H2O2} = 2 \times \ce{HO}$, $\ce{C6H12O6} = 6 \times \ce{CH2O}$ and
$\ce{C2H4} = 2 \times \ce{CH2}$.

**Where marks are lost.** Giving $\ce{CHO}$ for glucose by dividing only the carbon and
oxygen subscripts.

### 17. [Ch. 7] Period and group — 3 marks

**Strategy.** A period is the highest occupied shell; a main-group number follows from
the valence electron count.

**Working.** Sodium ($Z = 11$) is in period 3, group 1; chlorine ($Z = 17$) is in
period 3, group 17; calcium ($Z = 20$) is in period 4, group 2; neon ($Z = 10$) is in
period 2, group 18.

**Answer.** (a) Sodium: period 3, group 1; (b) chlorine: period 3, group 17;
(c) calcium: period 4, group 2; (d) neon: period 2, group 18.

**Check.** The period number equals the highest principal quantum number occupied, and
the group number matches the valence count for main-group elements.

**Where marks are lost.** Placing calcium in period 3 because its $Z$ is 20 and "the
third period ends at 20".

### 18. [Ch. 7] Symbols and Latin names — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Recall the symbols that do not follow the English name, and pair each
with its Latin root.

**Working.** Iron is Fe (ferrum), copper is Cu (cuprum), lead is Pb (plumbum), silver
is Ag (argentum), tungsten is W (wolfram) and potassium is K (kalium).

**Answer.** (a) Fe, iron, *ferrum*; (b) Cu, copper, *cuprum*; (c) Pb, lead,
*plumbum*; (d) Ag, silver, *argentum*; (e) W, tungsten, *wolfram*; (f) K, potassium,
*kalium*.

**Check.** Each symbol has one capital letter and, where present, one lowercase letter;
none contains a full stop or a space.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing "K" as "P" for potassium, or "W" as "T" for tungsten.

### 19. [Ch. 8] Counting atoms — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Treat each bracketed group as a unit and multiply by its subscript and by
any coefficient in front.

**Working.** $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$ contains 2 Al, 3 S and $3 \times 4 = 12$ O. In
$\ce{3Ca3(PO4)2}$ one formula unit contains 3 Ca, 2 P and 8 O; multiplying by 3 gives
9 Ca, 6 P and 24 O.

**Answer.** (a) $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$: 2 Al, 3 S, 12 O, 17 atoms in total.
(b) $\ce{3Ca3(PO4)2}$: 9 Ca, 6 P, 24 O, 39 atoms in total.

**Check.** The total counts agree with the sums of the individual element counts.

**Where marks are lost.** Forgetting that the subscript outside the bracket multiplies
every atom inside it.

### 20. [Ch. 9] Significant figures — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Apply the five rules: non-zero digits count; internal zeros count;
leading zeros never count; trailing zeros after a decimal point count; trailing zeros
in a whole number are ambiguous.

**Working.** $0.00450$ has three significant figures (two leading zeros do not count,
the trailing zero does). $1200$ with two significant figures intended has two. $2.500$
has four. $1.20 \times 10^{3}$ has three. $100.0$ has four.

**Answer.** (a) 3; (b) 2; (c) 4; (d) 3; (e) 4.

**Check.** Rewriting $1200$ as $1.2 \times 10^{3}$ makes the two-figure intention
explicit.

**Where marks are lost.** Counting the leading zeros of $0.00450$ as significant.

### 21. [Ch. 9] Unit conversion — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Multiply by conversion factors equal to one, arranged so that the
unwanted units cancel.

**Working.**
$$
72.0\ \text{km h}^{-1} \times \frac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}}
\times \frac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} = 20.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}
$$
$$
3.50\ \text{kg} \times \frac{10^{6}\ \text{mg}}{1\ \text{kg}}
= 3.50 \times 10^{6}\ \text{mg}
$$

**Answer.** (a) $20.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}$; (b) $3.50 \times 10^{6}\ \text{mg}$.

**Check.** $72\ \text{km h}^{-1}$ is about $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, which is a plausible
road speed.

**Where marks are lost.** Dividing by $1000$ instead of $3600$ in the time conversion,
which gives $72\,000\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.

### 22. [Ch. 10] Equipment and PPE — 3 marks

**Strategy.** Match the accuracy required to the calibration of the instrument; treat
PPE as the last line of defence.

**Working.** A bulb pipette delivers one fixed volume to high precision. A measuring
cylinder is graduated for approximate volumes. A volumetric flask is calibrated to
contain one fixed volume at a stated temperature. PPE protects the wearer but does not
remove the hazard.

**Answer.** (a) A bulb pipette (or a burette); (b) a measuring cylinder; (c) a
volumetric flask. One limitation of PPE is that it is the last line of defence: gloves
are permeable to some substances and can fail, and goggles do not protect against
inhalation, so engineering controls such as a fume hood are still required.

**Check.** The volumetric flask is used for the final make-up to the mark, not for
measuring the stock volume, which is delivered by pipette.

**Where marks are lost.** Specifying a beaker for an accurate $25.0\ \text{cm}^{3}$
volume.

## Part C — Intermediate problems

### 23. [Ch. 2] Particle diagrams — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Decide what each box contains: one kind of atom, two kinds joined in a
fixed ratio, or two substances not joined.

**Working.** Box A contains only one kind of circle, joined in pairs, so it represents
a diatomic element such as $\ce{O2}$. Box B contains two kinds of circle joined in a
$2:1$ ratio throughout, so it represents a compound such as $\ce{H2O}$. Box C contains
joined pairs of two colours plus separate single circles, so it contains a compound
and an element that are not chemically combined.

**Answer.** Box A is an element: all atoms are identical and are joined into
molecules. Box B is a compound: two elements are chemically bonded in a fixed $2:1$
ratio. Box C is a mixture: a compound and an element are present together without
chemical combination, and the proportions could vary.

**Check.** Only Box B has a composition that is fixed by the bonding; Boxes A and C can
contain different numbers of particles without changing their classification.

**Where marks are lost.** Calling Box C a compound because it contains joined pairs.

### 24. [Ch. 4] Zinc in hydrochloric acid — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Identify the products and compare with what simple dissolving would
produce.

**Working.** Zinc displaces hydrogen from the acid:
$\ce{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)}$. The gas is hydrogen, the solution
contains zinc chloride, and the reaction is exothermic. Evaporating the solution would
leave a white salt, not zinc.

**Answer.** The change is chemical because new substances are formed: zinc chloride in
solution and hydrogen gas. Two observations that distinguish it from dissolving are
the steady evolution of a gas that burns with a pop, and the fact that evaporation of
the final solution leaves a salt rather than recovering the metal. The warming of the
flask is consistent with an exothermic reaction but is not by itself decisive.

**Check.** If the zinc had merely dissolved, the metal could be recovered unchanged by
evaporation, and no gas would be produced.

**Where marks are lost.** Claiming that "the metal disappeared" is proof of a chemical
change; a metal can disappear by dissolving.

### 25. [Ch. 5] Separating sand, iron and salt — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Use one physical property per component: magnetism for iron, solubility
for salt, particle size for sand.

**Working.** Pass a magnet over the dry mixture to remove the iron filings. Add water
and stir so that the sodium chloride dissolves. Filter to collect the sand as the
residue. Evaporate the filtrate to recover the sodium chloride.

**Answer.** The order is: magnet, then dissolution, then filtration, then evaporation.
The order matters because removing the iron first prevents it from being trapped in the
sand or lost during filtration; dissolving before filtration is what separates the
soluble salt from the insoluble sand; and evaporation must come last so that the salt
is recovered from a solution that no longer contains sand.

**Check.** Every step uses a physical difference, so all three components are recovered
unchanged.

**Where marks are lost.** Evaporating the water before filtering, which leaves a
mixture of salt and sand.

### 26. [Ch. 5] Seawater and pure water — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Compare composition, separability and properties of components.

**Working.** Seawater contains water and dissolved salts in a proportion that varies
from place to place; evaporation or distillation separates it into pure water and a
mixture of salts. Pure water contains hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed $2:1$ atom ratio
and cannot be separated by a physical method.

**Answer.** Seawater is a mixture because its composition varies, its components
retain their individual properties, and it can be separated by evaporation or
distillation. Pure water is a compound because hydrogen and oxygen are chemically
combined in a fixed ratio — $11.19\%$ hydrogen and $88.81\%$ oxygen by mass — and no
physical process separates it into simpler substances. The properties also differ:
seawater conducts electricity and boils above $100\ ^\circ$ C, whereas pure water is a
poor conductor and boils sharply at $100\ ^\circ$ C at $1\ \text{atm}$.

**Check.** Electrolysis of water gives hydrogen and oxygen in a $2:1$ volume ratio,
which is a chemical decomposition, not a physical separation.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying seawater is a compound because "the salt is dissolved
in the water".

### 27. [Ch. 3] The melting plateau — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Distinguish average kinetic energy, which sets the temperature, from
potential energy, which changes during a phase transition.

**Working.** At the melting point the supplied energy is used to overcome the
attractive forces that hold the particles in the lattice. The energy increases the
potential energy of the particles rather than their average kinetic energy, and
temperature is a measure of average kinetic energy.

**Answer.** During melting, heat is supplied continuously but the temperature remains
constant because the energy is used to break the intermolecular attractions of the
lattice, not to increase the average kinetic energy of the particles. Temperature
measures average kinetic energy, so it does not rise while the potential energy
increases. Once all the solid has melted, further heating raises the kinetic energy and
the temperature of the liquid rises again.

**Check.** The length of the plateau is proportional to the mass of solid, which is
what the latent heat model predicts.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying that "the heat is lost", when in fact it is absorbed
as latent heat.

---

### 28. [Ch. 6] Ions and the formula of aluminium oxide — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Main-group atoms gain or lose electrons to reach the nearest noble-gas
configuration; the compound must be electrically neutral.

**Working.** Mg loses two electrons to give $\ce{Mg^2+}$; O gains two to give
$\ce{O^2-}$; Al loses three to give $\ce{Al^3+}$; Cl gains one to give $\ce{Cl-}$. For
aluminium oxide, the smallest whole-number ratio that balances $+3$ and $-2$ is two
$\ce{Al^3+}$ to three $\ce{O^2-}$.

**Answer.** The ions are $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{O^2-}$, $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$. The
formula of the compound between aluminium and oxygen is $\ce{Al2O3}$, because
$2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0$.

**Check.** The crossover rule gives the same result: the magnitude of each charge
becomes the subscript of the other ion, and $2:3$ is already the simplest ratio.

**Where marks are lost.** Writing $\ce{AlO}$ or $\ce{Al3O2}$; either would be charged.

### 29. [Ch. 7] Comparing ionisation energies — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Compare effective nuclear charge for atoms in the same shell, and compare
shell number and shielding for atoms in different periods.

**Working.** Sodium and magnesium both lose a $3s$ electron, but magnesium has one more
proton and the same shielding from inner shells, so its effective nuclear charge is
larger and its first ionisation energy is higher ($738$ against
$496\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$). Sodium and potassium both lose one $ns$ electron, but
potassium's electron is in $n = 4$, further from the nucleus and shielded by an extra
filled shell, so its ionisation energy is lower ($419$ against
$496\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$).

**Answer.** (a) Mg has the higher first ionisation energy because its valence electron
experiences a greater effective nuclear charge in the same $n = 3$ shell. (b) Na has
the higher first ionisation energy because its valence electron is in $n = 3$, whereas
potassium's is in the more shielded, more distant $n = 4$ shell.

**Check.** Both trends follow
$E_{\text{i}} \propto Z_{\text{eff}}^{2}/n^{2}$: raising $Z_{\text{eff}}$ raises the
energy, and raising $n$ lowers it.

**Where marks are lost.** Explaining the Na–Mg difference by atomic size alone,
without mentioning the extra proton.

### 30. [Ch. 8] Writing formulas — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Write the ions, then cross the charge magnitudes and reduce to the
simplest ratio.

**Working.** $\ce{Ca^2+}$ with $\ce{OH-}$ gives $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$. $\ce{Al^3+}$ with
$\ce{SO4^2-}$ gives $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$. $\ce{NH4+}$ with $\ce{NO3-}$ gives
$\ce{NH4NO3}$. $\ce{Mg^2+}$ with $\ce{Cl-}$ gives $\ce{MgCl2}$.

**Answer.** (a) $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$; (b) $\ce{Al2(SO4)3}$; (c) $\ce{NH4NO3}$;
(d) $\ce{MgCl2}$.

### 32. [Ch. 10] Risk score and controls — 4 marks

### 33. [Ch. 1] Burning magnesium at three levels — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Give one observation, one particle-level account and one equation.

**Working.** Macroscopically, magnesium burns with a brilliant white flame and leaves a
white ash. Submicroscopically, each magnesium atom loses two electrons to an oxygen
molecule, forming $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$ ions in an ionic lattice. Symbolically,
$$
\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}
$$

**Answer.** At the macroscopic level, a silvery ribbon burns with an intense white
light, releases heat and forms a white powder. At the submicroscopic level, magnesium
atoms are oxidised to $\ce{Mg^2+}$ ions while oxygen molecules are reduced to
$\ce{O^2-}$ ions, and the ions pack into a magnesium oxide lattice. At the symbolic
level, the reaction is $\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}$, which shows the conservation
of atoms and the $2:1$ combining ratio.

**Check.** The white product is magnesium oxide, not magnesium; the appearance of a new
substance is the macroscopic signature of the chemical change.

**Where marks are lost.** Describing only the observation and omitting the symbolic
level.

### 34. [Ch. 6] Why chlorine isotopes behave alike — 4 marks

**Strategy.** Separate the roles of protons, neutrons and electrons.

**Working.** Both isotopes have $Z = 17$ and therefore 17 electrons, with the same
electron configuration. Chemical behaviour is controlled by the valence electrons.
The isotopes differ only in neutron number, 18 against 20, so their masses differ.

**Answer.** $\ce{^{35}Cl}$ and $\ce{^{37}Cl}$ both have 17 protons and 17 electrons, so
they have identical electron configurations and form the same bonds; chemistry is
controlled by electrons, not by neutrons. They differ in mass because they contain 18
and 20 neutrons respectively, and the relative atomic mass of chlorine is the
abundance-weighted mean of these isotopic masses. The small mass difference does affect
diffusion rates and vibrational frequencies, which is why the chemical identity is
"almost" rather than absolutely exact.

**Check.** The relative atomic mass of chlorine, $35.45$, lies between 35 and 37, as a
weighted mean of the two isotopes must.

**Where marks are lost.** Saying the isotopes have "different numbers of electrons".

### 35. [Ch. 2, Ch. 5] Law of multiple proportions — 6 marks

**Strategy.** Convert the masses to amounts with $n = m/A_r$, then compare the amount
of oxygen that combines with a fixed amount of carbon.

**Working.** For carbon,
$$
n(\ce{C}) = \frac{1.000\ \text{g}}{12.011\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 0.08326\ \text{mol}
$$
For compound X,
$$
n(\ce{O}) = \frac{1.333\ \text{g}}{15.999\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 0.08332\ \text{mol},
\qquad \frac{n(\ce{O})}{n(\ce{C})} = 1.001 \approx 1
$$
For compound Y,
$$
n(\ce{O}) = \frac{2.667\ \text{g}}{15.999\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 0.16670\ \text{mol},
\qquad \frac{n(\ce{O})}{n(\ce{C})} = 2.002 \approx 2
$$

**Answer.** X is carbon monoxide, $\ce{CO}$, and Y is carbon dioxide, $\ce{CO2}$. For a
fixed mass of carbon, the masses of oxygen are in the ratio
$1.333 : 2.667 = 1 : 2$, a ratio of small whole numbers, which is the law of multiple
proportions: when two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element
that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a ratio of small whole numbers.

**Check.** The molar masses confirm the identification: $M(\ce{CO}) = 28.010$ and
$M(\ce{CO2}) = 44.009\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$, and the carbon mass fractions are $42.88\%$
and $27.29\%$, consistent with the analysed masses.

**Where marks are lost.** Comparing the masses of oxygen directly without dividing by
$A_r(\ce{O})$, or failing to fix the mass of carbon at $1.000\ \text{g}$.

### 36. [Ch. 4] A boundary case and radioactivity — 6 marks

**Strategy.** Apply the operational criterion — recovery of the original substance —
then distinguish nuclear from chemical change.

**Working.** The solid is recovered unchanged on evaporation, so no new substance has
been formed; the temperature fall is the endothermic enthalpy of solution, and the
conductivity arises from the mobility of the dissolved ions. Radioactivity, by
contrast, changes the nucleus: the element may transmute, and the electrons are left
largely untouched.

**Answer.** (a) The change is physical. The white solid is recovered unchanged when the
water evaporates, which shows that no new substance was formed; dissolution separates
the ions, and the conductivity is due to their mobility, not to a reaction. The
temperature fall is consistent with an endothermic process such as the dissolution of
ammonium nitrate. (b) Radioactivity is a nuclear change: the nucleus emits α or β
particles or γ radiation, and the element may be transmuted into a different element.
Chemical changes involve the rearrangement of electrons between nuclei, and the
nucleus itself is unaffected.

**Check.** Electrolysis of the recovered solid would be needed to decompose it, which
confirms that the dissolution itself was physical.

**Where marks are lost.** Classifying the dissolution as chemical because the solution
conducts electricity.

### 37. [Ch. 3] Why steam burns more severely — 6 marks

**Strategy.** Add the latent heat released on condensation to the sensible heat released
on cooling, using $q = mc\Delta T$ for the second step.

**Working.** The amount is
$$
n = \frac{18.015\ \text{g}}{18.015\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 1.000\ \text{mol}
$$
Condensation:
$$
q_1 = n\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = (1.000\ \text{mol})(40.7\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1})
= 40.7\ \text{kJ}
$$
Cooling:
$$
q_2 = mc\Delta T = (18.015\ \text{g})(4.18\ \text{J g}^{-1}\ \text{K}^{-1})(63.0\ \text{K})
= 4.74 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J} = 4.74\ \text{kJ}
$$
$$
q_{\text{total}} = 40.7 + 4.74 = 45.4\ \text{kJ}
$$

**Answer.** The steam releases $45.4\ \text{kJ}$ in total. Condensation contributes
$40.7/45.4 = 0.896$, or $89.6\%$, of the energy; cooling the resulting water from
$100$ to $37\ ^{\circ}$ C contributes only $10.4\%$. The latent heat of vaporisation is
therefore the reason a steam burn is far more severe than a burn from water at the same
temperature.

**Check.** Liquid water at $100\ ^{\circ}$ C would release only $4.74\ \text{kJ}$ on
cooling, so steam delivers about $9.6$ times as much energy.

**Where marks are lost.** Adding $40.7\ \text{kJ}$ to $4744\ \text{J}$ without
converting to the same unit, which gives a meaningless total.

### 42. [Ch. 5] Evaluating the claim about brass — 6 marks

**Strategy.** Test the claim against the defining criteria of a compound: fixed
composition by mass, chemical bonding between components, and properties that are not
those of the components.

**Working.** Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc. Its composition is variable — common
alloys range from about $60\%$ to $90\%$ copper — so it does not obey the law of
definite proportions. The copper and zinc atoms are mixed in a solid solution rather
than bonded in a fixed ratio, and the alloy retains metallic properties such as
conductivity and lustre.

**Answer.** The claim is false. Brass is a homogeneous mixture (a solid solution), not a
compound, because its composition can be varied continuously and does not obey a fixed
mass ratio. At the particle level, copper and zinc atoms occupy sites in the same
metallic lattice without chemical bonds between them in a definite proportion. The
claim confuses reproducible properties with fixed composition: brasses of different
composition have different properties, and a compound would have a sharp melting point
and a fixed formula.

**Check.** A compound such as $\ce{CuO}$ has a fixed $1:1$ atom ratio and properties
quite unlike those of copper and oxygen, which is exactly what brass lacks.

**Where marks are lost.** Agreeing that brass is a compound because it is a "pure"
material with a uniform appearance.
